Why go to Malaysia

Malaysia splits into two halves across the South China Sea: Peninsular Malaysia, home to Kuala Lumpur and most first-time visitors' itineraries, and East Malaysia on the island of Borneo, where Sabah and Sarawak hold rainforest, orangutans and Mount Kinabalu.

Its real strength is how three food and cultural traditions — Malay, Chinese and Indian — sit side by side rather than blended into one, so a single street in Penang or Kuala Lumpur can serve nasi lemak, char kway teow and roti canai within a few doors of each other.

Between the Petronas Towers, colonial-era hill stations, UNESCO-listed port towns and some of Southeast Asia's best diving, Malaysia rewards a trip built around contrast rather than a single theme.

Experiences

Best things to do in Malaysia

The experiences that recur across the country and make a Malaysia trip feel like more than a checklist of cities.

  1. Look up at the Petronas Towers

    An essential first stop in Kuala Lumpur, the world's tallest twin towers lit up above KLCC Park.

    Tip Book Skybridge and observation deck tickets online in advance — daily slots sell out, especially at sunset.

  2. Climb the steps at Batu Caves

    A limestone hill turned Hindu shrine just outside Kuala Lumpur, its rainbow-painted steps guarded by a towering gold statue.

    Tip Visit early morning to beat both the heat and the crowds climbing the 272 steps.

  3. Island-hop Langkawi or the Perhentians

    Malaysia's coastline and islands range from Langkawi's resorts to the Perhentians' backpacker beaches, with clear water throughout.

    Tip The east coast islands close to visitors during the November–February monsoon — plan around it.

  4. Wander George Town's street art

    Murals and wire-art sculptures scattered through Penang's UNESCO-listed old town, best explored slowly on foot.

    Tip Pick up a free street art map from a George Town visitor centre to track down the best-known pieces.

  5. Sip tea in the Cameron Highlands

    A cool-climate escape from the tropical heat, rolling tea plantations that feel transplanted from another country entirely.

    Tip Bring a light jacket — the highlands run noticeably cooler than anywhere else on a typical Malaysia itinerary.

  6. Eat late at a street food night market

    Malaysia's hawker culture is a genuine highlight in its own right, with entire streets given over to food stalls after dark.

    Tip Order in small portions from several stalls rather than filling up at the first one you find.

Food and drink

Malaysia foods to try

Malaysian food reflects the country's three main communities — Malay, Chinese and Indian — each keeping its own distinct dishes rather than blending into one cuisine.

  • Nasi lemak (Malaysia's national dish)

    Coconut milk rice served with sambal, fried anchovies, peanuts and a boiled or fried egg — eaten at any hour, including breakfast.

    Where to try it Roadside stalls and restaurants throughout the country.

  • Satay

    Skewered, grilled meat served with a rich peanut sauce, ubiquitous at night markets and hawker centres.

    Where to try it Night markets and hawker stalls nationwide.

  • Laksa

    A noodle soup with sharply distinct regional versions — Penang's sour, fish-based assam laksa and Sarawak's rich, spiced coconut version among the best known.

    Where to try it Hawker stalls in Penang, Sarawak and beyond.

  • Roti canai

    A flaky, pan-fried flatbread of Indian-Muslim origin, torn by hand and dipped in curry — a classic Malaysian breakfast.

    Where to try it Mamak stalls and restaurants throughout the country, many open late into the night.

  • Char kway teow

    Flat rice noodles stir-fried over high heat with prawns, egg, bean sprouts and Chinese sausage, a Penang specialty above all.

    Where to try it Hawker centres throughout Penang and the wider country.

  • Beef rendang

    Beef slow-cooked in coconut milk and spices until dark and richly concentrated, a centrepiece dish for celebrations.

    Where to try it Malay restaurants nationwide, especially around festive periods.

When to go

Best time to visit Malaysia

Malaysia is hot and humid year-round, but two separate monsoon seasons mean the best timing depends on which coast you're visiting.

  • December–February

    Best time

    Generally the driest window for Kuala Lumpur, Penang, Langkawi, Malacca and the Cameron Highlands.

  • March–October

    Best time

    The main season for the Perhentian Islands and Taman Negara's river access, both quieter or closed outside it.

  • May–September (southwest monsoon)

    Good

    Brings more rain to the west coast, though usually as short afternoon downpours rather than all-day rain.

  • November–February (northeast monsoon)

    Good

    Heavy rain and rough seas on the east coast Peninsula; the Perhentian Islands' resorts largely close for the season.

Worth planning around: Late November through January on the east coast Peninsula, when monsoon swells close most island resorts.

Trip length

How long to spend in Malaysia

Malaysia's split between the Peninsula and Borneo means a single trip rarely covers both comfortably.

  • 4–5 days

    Kuala Lumpur and Malacca or Penang as a focused introduction.

    Enough for the capital plus one historic city without feeling rushed.

  • 7–9 days

    Kuala Lumpur, Cameron Highlands and Penang.

    A well-paced route through Peninsular Malaysia's highlands and heritage coast.

  • 10–14 days

    The itinerary on this page: Kuala Lumpur, Malacca, Cameron Highlands, Ipoh, Penang and Langkawi.

    A complete first look at Peninsular Malaysia's cities, hills, heritage towns and islands.

  • 3 weeks or more

    Add a dedicated Borneo leg — Kota Kinabalu, Kuching, or the Perhentian Islands and Taman Negara.

    Sabah and Sarawak amount to a separate trip in practice, reached by their own domestic flights.

Getting around

Transportation in Malaysia

Domestic flights, trains and long-distance coaches together cover Malaysia well — the main decision is simply whether Borneo is part of the trip.

  • Domestic flights

    Essential for reaching Kota Kinabalu, Kuching or Langkawi quickly.

    Budget carriers connect Kuala Lumpur to nearly every corner of the country cheaply.

  • The KTM ETS high-speed rail

    Comfortable and efficient for the corridor linking Kuala Lumpur, Ipoh and Penang.

    An easy way to cover Peninsular Malaysia's west coast without driving.

  • Long-distance coaches

    Extensive and comfortable, reaching towns the train doesn't, including Malacca and the Cameron Highlands.

    Overnight services connect the Peninsula to the Perhentian Islands' jetty towns.

  • Renting a car

    Useful for exploring the Cameron Highlands or Langkawi at your own pace.

    Malaysia drives on the left, with generally good highways on the Peninsula.

  • Ferries

    The only way to reach Langkawi from the mainland by sea, or the Perhentian Islands from Kuala Besut.

    Frequent on popular routes, far less so outside the main season.

Culture

Culture and etiquette in Malaysia

Malaysian culture rests on three main communities — Malay, Chinese and Indian — each keeping distinct traditions, languages and holidays side by side.

  • Dress modestly at religious sites

    Cover shoulders and knees when visiting mosques and temples; some mosques provide robes for visitors.

  • Use your right hand

    Eating, giving or receiving items with the right hand is customary, especially in Malay and Indian contexts.

  • Remove shoes before entering

    Expected at homes, mosques and many temples — follow the lead of others at the entrance.

  • Greetings vary by community

    A handshake is common, though some Muslim women may not shake hands with men — wait to see if a hand is offered.

  • Ramadan affects daily rhythms

    During the fasting month, avoid eating or drinking in public in front of fasting Malay Muslims out of respect.

  • Public affection is best kept low-key

    Malaysia is culturally conservative in this respect, more so outside major cities.

Do

  • Try the food of all three communities — Malay, Chinese and Indian cuisines each stand on their own.
  • Use ride-hailing apps, which are cheap, reliable and widely used in every city.
  • Carry small cash for hawker stalls and markets, even in cities where cards are common.
  • Pack a light jacket for the Cameron Highlands, noticeably cooler than the rest of the country.

Don't

  • Don't point with your index finger — use your thumb instead, especially when gesturing at people.
  • Don't touch someone's head, considered disrespectful across Malay, Chinese and Indian traditions alike.
  • Don't assume alcohol is served everywhere — many Malay-run restaurants don't serve it.
  • Don't underestimate the East Coast monsoon — check seasonal closures before booking island time.

Travel tips

Malaysia travel tips

Practical notes that make travel through Malaysia noticeably smoother.

  • Book domestic flights early for routes to Sabah or Sarawak — prices climb fast closer to departure.
  • Pack a light jacket for the Cameron Highlands, even if the rest of the trip is tropical heat.
  • Carry small ringgit notes for hawker stalls, markets and short taxi rides.
  • Check East Coast monsoon dates before booking the Perhentian Islands or Taman Negara.
  • Use ride-hailing apps in cities rather than hailing taxis on the street.
  • Try street food confidently at busy stalls — high turnover is generally a good sign of freshness.
  • Carry a printed or downloaded map for the Cameron Highlands and Taman Negara, where signal can be patchy.
  • Dress modestly when visiting mosques, temples and more conservative small towns.

FAQ

Visiting Malaysia: frequently asked questions

How many days do you need in Malaysia?

Ten to fourteen days covers Kuala Lumpur and Peninsular Malaysia's highlands, heritage towns and islands well, which is the itinerary in this guide. A dedicated Borneo trip is best planned separately.

Is Malaysia expensive?

No, generally affordable by international standards, with hawker food, buses and trains all reasonably priced even on a modest budget.

Do you need a car in Malaysia?

No, not for the main Peninsular route — trains, coaches and domestic flights cover it well, though a car helps in the Cameron Highlands.

Can you visit Peninsular Malaysia and Borneo in one trip?

You can, but it requires its own domestic flight and adds significant time — most first-time visitors focus on one or the other.

When should you avoid the Perhentian Islands?

November through February, when the northeast monsoon brings rough seas and most island resorts close for the season.

Is Malaysia safe?

Yes, generally — a safe country to travel in, with petty theft in crowded tourist areas the main thing to watch for.
