Welcome to Dresden

Dresden was, before February 1945, known as 'Elbflorenz' — the Florence of the Elbe — for a baroque old town considered among the most beautiful in Europe. Over three nights that February, Allied bombing raids created a firestorm that destroyed roughly 90% of the city centre and killed an estimated 25,000 people, one of the most devastating single events of the war in Europe.

What makes Dresden genuinely remarkable to visit today is the completeness of its reconstruction. The Frauenkirche, left as a deliberate ruin by the East German government as an anti-war memorial, was rebuilt after reunification using as many original blackened stones as could be salvaged, finally reconsecrated in 2005 — its facade still visibly patchworked between original dark stone and new pale stone, a genuinely moving record of what was lost and recovered.

The wider old town — the Zwinger Palace, the Semperoper opera house, the Residenzschloss — has been rebuilt with similar care, and Dresden's museums hold some of Germany's finest art and treasure collections, assembled by the Saxon electors who ruled from here for centuries before the war ever touched the city.

Why visit

Why visit Dresden?

Dresden's case is that no other German city tells the story of wartime destruction and careful, genuine reconstruction this completely or this movingly.

  • The reconstruction is a genuine, ongoing story

    The Frauenkirche's patchworked stone facade makes the rebuilding process itself visible, not hidden behind a uniform new finish.

  • The baroque architecture is genuinely world class

    The Zwinger and Semperoper rank among Europe's finest baroque buildings, rebuilt with real fidelity to the originals.

  • The art collections are a real, underrated strength

    Dresden's museums hold masterworks and royal treasures that punch well above what the city's size might suggest.

  • It is genuinely more affordable than western German cities

    Dresden offers a comparable quality of sights and dining at noticeably lower cost than Munich or Cologne.

  • The Elbe riverside setting is genuinely beautiful

    The old town's baroque skyline reflected in the river is one of Germany's most photographed urban views.

Main attractions

Top things to see in Dresden

Dresden's essential sights, concentrated tightly enough in the old town to cover comfortably over two or three days.

  1. 01

    The Frauenkirche

    Dresden's rebuilt landmark church, its facade a visible patchwork of salvaged blackened stones and new pale ones, reconsecrated in 2005 after decades as a ruin.

    Tip Climb the dome for panoramic views over the old town and the Elbe, genuinely worth the ticket price.

  2. 02

    The Zwinger Palace

    A baroque palace complex of ornate pavilions around a central courtyard, now housing several major museums including the Old Masters Picture Gallery.

    Tip The Old Masters Gallery holds Raphael's Sistine Madonna, one of the collection's genuine highlights.

  3. 03

    The Semperoper

    Dresden's opera house, a baroque revival landmark on Theaterplatz, genuinely one of the world's most acoustically celebrated opera venues.

    Tip Guided daytime tours let you see the interior even without attending a performance.

  4. 04

    The Green Vault (Grünes Gewölbe)

    One of Europe's oldest and richest treasure collections, housed in the Residenzschloss, displaying jewels, gold and ivory work of genuinely startling craftsmanship.

    Tip The Historic Green Vault has strictly limited timed entry — book well ahead, especially in summer.

  5. 05

    The Fürstenzug (Procession of Princes)

    A 102-metre mural of Saxon rulers on horseback, made of over 23,000 Meissen porcelain tiles, remarkably undamaged by the 1945 bombing.

    Tip The tiles' scale is only fully apparent walking its full length slowly rather than viewing from one spot.

  6. 06

    The Brühl's Terrace

    A raised riverside promenade above the Elbe, nicknamed 'the Balcony of Europe', offering the classic view over the old town's baroque skyline.

    Tip Sunset is genuinely the best time, when the terrace's own buildings and the river both catch the light.

Things to do

Best things to do in Dresden

Beyond touring the baroque old town, Dresden rewards a slower pace along the river and into its contrasting modern quarter.

  1. Take an Elbe river cruise

    Historic paddle steamers, some over a century old, run scenic routes along the Elbe, giving a genuinely different view of the rebuilt skyline from the water.

    Tip The Dresden paddle steamer fleet is the world's oldest and largest still in operation, a genuine attraction in itself.

  2. Explore the Neustadt district

    Across the river from the baroque old town, the Neustadt survived the bombing largely intact and now holds Dresden's alternative, creative, nightlife-focused scene.

    Tip The Kunsthofpassage, a set of themed courtyards with quirky facades, is a genuinely fun, free wander.

  3. Visit the Dresden Christmas market (Striezelmarkt)

    Germany's oldest documented Christmas market, running since 1434, genuinely one of the country's most historic and atmospheric.

    Tip The market's giant Stollen cake, paraded and then cut on Stollenfest, is a genuine only-in-Dresden spectacle if timing allows.

  4. Day trip to Saxon Switzerland

    A national park of dramatic sandstone formations and table mountains, genuinely striking scenery within about an hour of the city.

    Tip The Bastei bridge viewpoint is the park's most famous and most photographed spot, reachable by a manageable walk.

  5. Visit the Military History Museum

    A genuinely provocative museum housed in a former arsenal, its building deliberately split by a dramatic modern wedge-shaped extension, addressing German military history with real critical honesty.

    Tip Allow at least two hours — the museum's thematic, rather than chronological, layout rewards unhurried exploration.

  6. Walk or cycle along the Elbe meadows

    Unlike many German cities, Dresden's riverbanks remain largely undeveloped meadowland, a genuinely relaxed green space right against the historic centre.

    Tip The path toward the Blue Wonder bridge in Loschwitz makes a pleasant longer walk or cycle from the centre.

Local food

What to eat in Dresden

Saxon food centres on hearty regional cooking and a few genuinely famous local specialities, Stollen above all.

  • Dresdner Stollen

    A dense, fruit-and-marzipan Christmas bread dusted heavily in sugar, with a protected designation of origin specifically for Dresden-made versions.

    Where to try it Bakeries across the city, genuinely best in the weeks around Christmas though sold year-round.

  • Sächsischer Sauerbraten

    Saxony's own version of the marinated pot roast, traditionally made with a distinctive gingerbread-thickened gravy unlike other regional versions.

    Where to try it Traditional Saxon restaurants across the old town and Neustadt.

  • Eierschecke

    A layered Saxon cake of yeast dough, curd cheese and an egg custard topping, a genuine regional specialty found almost nowhere else in Germany.

    Where to try it Traditional Konditorei cake shops throughout Dresden.

  • Quarkkeulchen

    Sweet fried curd cheese patties, traditionally served with apple sauce and cinnamon sugar, a genuine Saxon comfort food.

    Where to try it Traditional restaurants and cafes across the city.

  • Saxon wine

    One of Germany's smallest and most northerly wine regions, along the Elbe valley near Dresden, producing genuinely distinctive wines from a UNESCO-recognised terraced landscape.

    Where to try it Wine taverns in the city and vineyard visits in the nearby Elbe wine villages.

  • Bautzner Senf (mustard)

    A genuinely famous regional mustard from nearby Bautzen, a real Saxon pantry staple used across the region's cooking.

    Where to try it Grocery shops and traditional restaurants throughout Saxony.

Accommodation

Where to stay in Dresden

Dresden's compact old town means most central accommodation puts you within walking distance of the main sights.

  • The Altstadt (Old Town)

    The most central base, though accommodation directly within the historic core is limited and comes at a premium.

    Best for: Walking to the Frauenkirche and Zwinger

  • The Neustadt

    A short walk across the river, with good value accommodation and easy access to Dresden's alternative dining and nightlife scene.

    Best for: Nightlife and a livelier, more affordable base

  • Near the Hauptbahnhof

    A short walk or tram ride from the old town, with a good range of mid-range accommodation.

    Best for: Transport convenience

Transport

Getting to and around Dresden

Dresden Airport handles limited direct international traffic, making Berlin a common secondary gateway.

Getting there

  • Dresden Airport (DRS)

    Connected to the city centre by tram in about 25 minutes, with a smaller range of direct international routes than Germany's major hubs.

    A practical option when direct flights are available, though many visitors arrive via Berlin instead.

  • Rail from Berlin

    Direct trains connect Berlin to Dresden in under 2 hours, a genuinely easy and popular route.

    Book Sparpreis fares as early as possible for significant savings over walk-up tickets.

  • Rail from Prague

    Direct trains connect Dresden to Prague in about 2 hours, following the scenic Elbe Valley, a popular route for combining both cities.

    The Elbe Valley stretch of this route is considered genuinely scenic in its own right.

Getting around

Dresden's old town is comfortably walkable, and an efficient tram network covers the wider city well.

  • Walking

    The Altstadt's major sights sit within a compact, walkable centre, easily covered on foot.

    Comfortable shoes matter for cobbled streets throughout the historic core.

  • Trams

    Dresden's extensive tram network reaches the Neustadt, the Hauptbahnhof and the wider city efficiently.

    A single ticket system covers trams and buses within the same fare zone.

  • Elbe paddle steamers

    The historic steamer fleet doubles as scenic transport between the old town and outlying riverside districts like Loschwitz.

    A genuinely pleasant, if slower, alternative to trams for a riverside-focused day.

When to go

Best time to visit Dresden

Dresden's weather is genuinely continental, with warmer summers and colder winters than western Germany, making the shoulder months the most reliable window.

  • Spring (April–June)

    Best time

    Mild and increasingly pleasant, with the Elbe meadows at their greenest and crowds still below summer peak.

  • Summer (July–August)

    Good

    Warm, sometimes genuinely hot, weather ideal for the Elbe meadows and Saxon Switzerland day trips, though the busiest tourist season.

  • Autumn (September–October)

    Best time

    Comfortable temperatures, thinning crowds, and good light for photographing the baroque skyline from Brühl's Terrace.

  • Winter (November–March)

    Good

    The Striezelmarkt Christmas market brings genuine historic atmosphere, and a snow-dusted old town is a real sight, though days are short and cold.

Worth planning around: February's anniversary of the 1945 bombing is treated with real civic solemnity — an interesting time to witness Dresden's memorial culture, but not the liveliest for a typical visit.

Itinerary

Three days in Dresden

The baroque old town first, then museums and the Neustadt, then a day trip into the surrounding Saxon countryside.

  1. Day1

    The baroque old town

    Dresden's rebuilt landmarks.

    • Morning The Frauenkirche, including the dome climb.
    • Afternoon The Zwinger Palace and Brühl's Terrace at golden hour.
    • Evening Dinner in the old town.
  2. Day2

    Treasures and the Neustadt

    Dresden's museums and its alternative side.

    • Morning The Green Vault, timed tickets permitting.
    • Afternoon Cross the river to explore the Neustadt and Kunsthofpassage.
    • Evening Dinner and nightlife in the Neustadt.
  3. Day3

    Saxon Switzerland

    A day trip into dramatic sandstone countryside.

    • Morning Travel to Saxon Switzerland National Park.
    • Afternoon The Bastei bridge and surrounding viewpoints.
    • Evening Return to Dresden for a final dinner.

Travel tips

Dresden travel tips

Practical notes on booking, timing and day trips that make a real difference to a Dresden trip.

  • Book Green Vault tickets online well ahead — timed entry is strictly limited and sells out, especially in summer.
  • Climb the Frauenkirche dome for genuinely worthwhile panoramic views over the old town and river.
  • Visit Brühl's Terrace at sunset for the best light over the baroque skyline.
  • Allow a full day for Saxon Switzerland if visiting — it's a genuine day trip, not a quick add-on.
  • Cross to the Neustadt for noticeably better-value dining than the more touristed old town.
  • Book Dresden accommodation well ahead during the Striezelmarkt Christmas market period in December.
  • Wear comfortable shoes — much of the old town's charm is in slow walking over cobbled streets.

FAQ

Dresden: frequently asked questions

How many days do you need in Dresden?

Two to three. Two covers the baroque old town's essential sights; three allows a proper day trip into Saxon Switzerland without feeling rushed.

Is Dresden's old town original or rebuilt?

Almost entirely rebuilt after 1945's devastating bombing, though with genuine care and, in the Frauenkirche's case, using as many salvaged original stones as possible — the reconstruction itself is a large part of what makes visiting worthwhile.

What is the best time of year to visit?

May to June and September offer the best balance of good weather and manageable crowds. Winter brings the historic Striezelmarkt Christmas market; summer is warmest and best for day trips into Saxon Switzerland.

Is Dresden worth visiting as a day trip from Berlin?

It's possible at under two hours each way by train, but a rushed single day only covers the old town's highlights — an overnight stay allows time for the Neustadt and a Saxon Switzerland day trip as well.

Is Dresden expensive?

Genuinely more affordable than western German cities like Munich or Cologne, for a comparable quality of museums, dining and accommodation.
