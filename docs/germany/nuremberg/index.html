Welcome to Nuremberg

Nuremberg was, for centuries, the closest thing the Holy Roman Empire had to an unofficial capital — newly elected emperors traditionally held their first Imperial Diet here, and the Kaiserburg castle above the old town still carries that imperial weight in its architecture. Albrecht Dürer, the Renaissance's great German artist, lived and worked in the city, and its medieval old town was, before 1945, considered one of the best preserved in Germany.

That old town was almost entirely destroyed in a single 1945 bombing raid, and rebuilt afterward with genuine care to recreate its medieval character, red-roofed houses and city walls largely intact in appearance if not in original stone. The reconstruction is convincing enough that first-time visitors often assume they are looking at genuinely centuries-old buildings.

Nuremberg's 20th-century history sits alongside this medieval identity with real, deliberate honesty. The city was chosen by the Nazi Party for its massive annual rallies specifically because of its historic imperial associations, and after the war, the Allied powers chose Nuremberg for the trials of Nazi leadership for the same symbolic reason. Both the rally grounds and the trial courtroom remain, preserved and interpreted rather than erased.

Why visit

Why visit Nuremberg?

Nuremberg's case is that no other German city holds this much genuine medieval imperial history alongside such a direct, unflinching reckoning with the 20th century.

  • The old town's reconstruction is genuinely convincing

    Rebuilt with real care after near-total destruction, it captures medieval Nuremberg's character more completely than expected.

  • The Nazi-era history is presented with real honesty

    The Rally Grounds documentation centre and the Nuremberg Trials courtroom both address this history directly rather than around it.

  • The imperial history is genuinely significant

    The Kaiserburg and the city's historic role in the Holy Roman Empire give Nuremberg a weight few German cities its size can match.

  • Its Christmas market is genuinely one of the world's best known

    The Christkindlesmarkt draws visitors from across the world and has real, centuries-old roots in the city.

  • It works well as both a destination and a base

    The city rewards two focused days alone, and sits well within reach of the wider Franconia region for those with more time.

Main attractions

Top things to see in Nuremberg

Nuremberg's essential sights, spanning its medieval imperial history and its direct, honest 20th-century reckoning.

  1. 01

    The Kaiserburg (Imperial Castle)

    A fortified castle complex above the old town, used by Holy Roman Emperors for centuries, with genuinely commanding views over Nuremberg's red rooftops.

    Tip The Sinwell Tower climb offers the best panoramic view over the whole old town.

  2. 02

    Nuremberg's Old Town

    Rebuilt after near-total wartime destruction, with the Hauptmarkt square, the Schöner Brunnen fountain and the Pegnitz river's half-timbered houses recreating medieval character convincingly.

    Tip The Schöner Brunnen fountain has a hidden golden ring in its railing, said to bring luck if spun three times.

  3. 03

    The Nazi Party Rally Grounds and Documentation Centre

    The vast unfinished grounds where Nazi Party rallies were staged, now housing a documentation centre that presents this history with real, unflinching directness.

    Tip The audio guide is genuinely worth the extra cost, providing context the exhibits alone don't fully convey.

  4. 04

    The Nuremberg Trials Courtroom (Memorium)

    Courtroom 600, where the Nuremberg Trials of Nazi leadership took place, preserved and open to visitors alongside a museum on the trials' history and legal legacy.

    Tip The courtroom is still in occasional use for real legal proceedings — check ahead, as this can affect visitor access.

  5. 05

    St. Lorenz Church

    A Gothic masterpiece and one of Nuremberg's two great medieval churches, its facade largely surviving the 1945 bombing that destroyed most of the surrounding old town.

    Tip The interior's Tabernacle and Angelic Salutation sculpture are genuine Gothic masterworks worth seeking out.

  6. 06

    The Germanisches Nationalmuseum

    Germany's largest museum of cultural history, spanning prehistory to the present, with genuinely significant collections including works by Nuremberg's own Albrecht Dürer.

    Tip The museum's scale rewards a focused visit to specific eras rather than attempting the whole collection in one go.

Things to do

Best things to do in Nuremberg

Beyond touring the old town and its 20th-century history sites, Nuremberg rewards its food traditions and Franconian surroundings.

  1. Eat Nuremberg's famous small bratwurst

    Nuremberg's sausages are a genuine protected regional specialty, smaller than most German bratwurst, traditionally grilled over beechwood and served three, six or a dozen at a time.

    Tip Bratwurst Röslein and Bratwursthäusle are both long-running, genuinely traditional spots near the old town.

  2. Walk the old town's surviving city walls

    Substantial sections of Nuremberg's medieval walls and towers survive, walkable for free and offering a genuine sense of the old town's original fortified scale.

    Tip The stretch near the Königstor gate is among the most complete and atmospheric.

  3. Visit the Nuremberg Christmas market (Christkindlesmarkt)

    One of the world's most famous Christmas markets, running since the 16th century, with the Hauptmarkt filled with stalls, mulled wine and Nuremberg's own gingerbread.

    Tip The market opens with a ceremonial address from the 'Christkind' — worth timing a visit around if the opening ceremony aligns with your trip.

  4. Take a Dürer house tour

    Albrecht Dürer's preserved home and workshop, where the Renaissance master lived and worked, gives genuine insight into his life and working methods.

    Tip Living-history costumed guides run some sessions, adding real character to the standard tour.

  5. Day trip into Franconian wine country or Bamberg

    The wider Franconia region around Nuremberg holds its own wine country and Bamberg, a genuinely stunning, largely undamaged medieval town within easy reach.

    Tip Bamberg's own UNESCO-listed old town makes a genuinely worthwhile full-day trip from Nuremberg.

  6. Visit the Nuremberg Toy Museum

    A genuinely charming museum tracing Nuremberg's centuries-long toy-making history, the city having been a major European toy manufacturing centre for generations.

    Tip The historic toy collection appeals to adults with an interest in design history as much as to children.

Local food

What to eat in Nuremberg

Franconian food centres on Nuremberg's famous small sausages, gingerbread and hearty regional cooking.

  • Nürnberger Rostbratwurst

    Small, finger-sized grilled sausages with a protected geographical status, traditionally grilled over beechwood charcoal and served three, six or a dozen at a time.

    Where to try it Traditional Bratwurst restaurants throughout the old town, several centuries old.

  • Nürnberger Lebkuchen

    Nuremberg's famous gingerbread, a protected regional specialty made with honey, nuts and spices rather than flour-heavy dough, genuinely distinct from ordinary gingerbread.

    Where to try it Traditional bakeries across the city, especially plentiful around the Christmas market season.

  • Schäufele

    A roasted pork shoulder, a genuine Franconian classic, typically served with a bread dumpling and rich gravy.

    Where to try it Traditional Franconian Gasthaus restaurants throughout Nuremberg.

  • Franconian beer

    Franconia has the highest density of breweries in the world relative to its population, producing genuinely distinctive, often unfiltered local beer styles.

    Where to try it Traditional Nuremberg beer cellars and Franconian breweries, several offering their own house styles.

  • Karpfen (carp)

    A traditional Franconian dish, particularly around Christmas and New Year, from the region's historic fish-pond farming tradition dating back centuries.

    Where to try it Traditional Franconian restaurants, especially in winter when the dish is most traditionally served.

  • Nürnberger Bratwurst breakfast

    A genuinely local tradition: a small portion of the city's famous bratwurst eaten as a savoury breakfast, sometimes with a beer alongside.

    Where to try it Historic Bratwurst taverns in the old town, particularly popular with locals in the morning.

Accommodation

Where to stay in Nuremberg

Nuremberg's compact old town means most central accommodation puts you within walking distance of the main sights.

  • The Altstadt (Old Town)

    The most central base, putting the castle, old town and main historic sights all within easy walking distance.

    Best for: Walking to the Kaiserburg and Hauptmarkt

  • Near the Hauptbahnhof

    A short walk from the old town's southern gate, with a good range of mid-range accommodation.

    Best for: Transport convenience

  • Gostenhof

    A genuinely up-and-coming neighbourhood just west of the old town, with good restaurants and a younger crowd.

    Best for: A livelier, more local atmosphere

Transport

Getting to and around Nuremberg

Nuremberg Airport handles a reasonable range of European routes, with excellent rail connections across Germany.

Getting there

  • Nuremberg Airport (NUE)

    Connected to the city centre by U-Bahn in about 12 minutes, a genuinely convenient, close airport.

    A smaller range of direct international routes than Germany's largest hubs, though generally sufficient for European connections.

  • Rail from other German cities

    ICE trains connect Nuremberg to Munich in about 1 hour, and Berlin in about 3h15.

    Book Sparpreis fares as early as possible for significant savings over walk-up tickets.

  • Rental car

    Useful for reaching Bamberg and the wider Franconia region, though not required for the city itself.

    Nuremberg's old town is largely pedestrianised, making a car more useful for day trips than the city centre.

Getting around

Nuremberg's old town is comfortably walkable, and an efficient U-Bahn and tram network covers the wider city.

  • Walking

    The Altstadt's major sights sit within a compact, largely pedestrianised centre, easily covered on foot.

    Comfortable shoes matter for cobbled streets throughout the historic core.

  • U-Bahn and trams

    Nuremberg's public transport network efficiently connects the old town to the airport, station and outlying districts.

    A single ticket system covers the U-Bahn, trams and buses within the same fare zone.

  • Rental car

    Useful specifically for day trips to Bamberg or Franconia's wine country beyond the city.

    Parking within the old town's walls is limited — park-and-ride options exist on the city's edge.

When to go

Best time to visit Nuremberg

Nuremberg's weather is genuinely continental, and the Christmas market season is a genuine draw on its own that shapes when many visitors choose to come.

  • Spring (April–June)

    Best time

    Mild and increasingly pleasant, with crowds well below the Christmas market's December peak.

  • Summer (July–August)

    Good

    Warm weather good for exploring the old town and day trips to Bamberg, though also a busier tourist season.

  • Autumn (September–October)

    Best time

    Comfortable temperatures and thinning crowds, a genuinely good window before the Christmas market rush begins.

  • Winter (November–December)

    Good

    The Christkindlesmarkt runs from late November through Christmas Eve, bringing genuine world-famous atmosphere, though also the year's busiest, most expensive weeks.

Worth planning around: December weekends without booked accommodation, when the Christmas market draws visitors from across the world and prices and crowds both spike accordingly.

Itinerary

Three days in Nuremberg

The medieval old town first, then the city's direct 20th-century history, then Franconian food and a Bamberg day trip.

  1. Day1

    The old town and Kaiserburg

    Nuremberg's imperial and medieval history.

    • Morning The Kaiserburg and Sinwell Tower.

    20th-century history

    Bamberg day trip

    A day trip to Franconia's other great historic town.

Travel tips

Nuremberg travel tips

Practical notes on booking, timing and history sites that make a real difference to a Nuremberg trip.

  • Book Christmas market period accommodation many months ahead if visiting in December.
  • Allow at least half a day for the Documentation Centre and Rally Grounds — the history deserves unhurried time.
  • Check Courtroom 600's visitor access ahead, since it occasionally hosts real legal proceedings.
  • Climb the Sinwell Tower at the Kaiserburg for the best panoramic view over the old town's red rooftops.
  • Try Nürnberger sausages at a historic tavern rather than a tourist-strip stall for a genuinely better version.
  • Consider a Bamberg day trip if staying three or more days — it's a genuinely worthwhile, easy addition.
  • Wear comfortable shoes — much of the old town's charm is in slow walking over cobbled streets.

FAQ

Nuremberg: frequently asked questions

How many days do you need in Nuremberg?

Two to three. Two covers the old town and the essential Nazi-era history sites; three allows a Bamberg day trip without feeling rushed.

Is Nuremberg's old town original or rebuilt?

Almost entirely rebuilt after near-total destruction in a 1945 bombing raid, though with genuine care to recreate its medieval character — convincing enough that many visitors assume it's original.

Is the Nazi-era history difficult to visit?

It's substantial and genuinely sobering, but presented with real honesty and educational care rather than being glossed over — most visitors find it a valuable, worthwhile part of understanding the city and the period.

What is the best time of year to visit?

May to June and September offer the best weather and manageable crowds for general sightseeing. December's Christkindlesmarkt is genuinely world-famous but also the busiest, most expensive time to visit.

Is Nuremberg worth visiting beyond the Christmas market?

Yes, genuinely — the Kaiserburg, the rebuilt old town, the Nazi-era history sites and the Germanisches Nationalmuseum are all worthwhile independent of the market, which runs only from late November to Christmas Eve.
