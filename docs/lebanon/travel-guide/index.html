Lebanon packs Phoenician ports, the largest Roman temples ever built, Crusader and Mamluk cities, cedar forests, cliffside monasteries and one of the great cuisines of the Middle East into a country that you can cross in a morning. It is also a country in a difficult moment, with a collapsed economy and a recent war. This guide covers how the regions divide, how to choose between the ten destinations, how to manage money and safety, when to go and three routes that work.

How Lebanon divides

Lebanon is about 210 km from north to south and 45 to 80 km wide, and it is made up of four parallel strips: the coastal plain, the Mount Lebanon range, the Bekaa Valley and the Anti-Lebanon range along the Syrian border. Almost everything visitors see falls into one of five regions.

Beirut and its surroundings. The capital, Beirut, is a dense, lively and scarred city that is the base for most trips. To the north of it, Jeita Grotto and Harissa are easy half-day trips.

The north coast. Byblos and Batroun are small coastal towns with Phoenician and Crusader remains, and beaches and restaurants. Further north, Tripoli is the country's second city, with a well-preserved Mamluk old town.

Mount Lebanon. The mountains behind the coast hold the Qadisha Valley and the Cedars in the north, and the Chouf, with Beiteddine Palace, Deir al-Qamar and the Chouf Cedar Reserve, in the south.

The south. Sidon and Tyre are ancient Phoenician ports with Crusader and Roman remains. This is the area closest to the Israeli border, and the one where advice has been strictest since 2023.

The Bekaa Valley. A high, fertile plain between two mountain ranges, with the temples of Baalbek, the Umayyad city of Anjar and most of Lebanon's vineyards. Parts of the valley near the Syrian border have been affected by strikes.

Worth knowing: The practical unit is a loop: Beirut, the north coast, the mountains, and then, depending on advice, the south and the Bekaa. Almost everything is a day trip from somewhere.

Choosing between the ten destinations

  • If you have three or four days

    Beirut with day trips to Byblos (Jbeil) and Jeita Grotto. The capital and the nearest coast can be done with a driver and no long transfers.

  • If you care about Roman and ancient history

    Baalbek for the temples, Byblos (Jbeil) and Tyre (Sour) for the Phoenician cities, and Anjar & the Bekaa Wine Country for the Umayyad city. Check current advice for the Bekaa and the south.

  • If you like castles and old cities

    Tripoli for its Crusader citadel and Mamluk quarters, Sidon (Saida) for its sea castle and souks, and Byblos (Jbeil) for its Crusader castle.

  • If you like mountains and walking

    The Qadisha Valley and the Cedars in the north, and the Chouf Cedar Reserve near Beiteddine.

  • If you want food, wine and nightlife

    Beirut for restaurants and bars, Batroun for the coast and its wineries, and the Bekaa for wine.

  • If you want a beach

    Batroun has the most attractive stretch of coast within easy reach of the capital, and Byblos (Jbeil) has small beaches near the old town.

Money, power and daily life since the crisis

Since 2019, Lebanon has been through one of the worst economic collapses of modern times. The Lebanese pound has lost more than 90% of its value, banks have restricted access to savings, and the country's public electricity supply provides only a few hours a day in many places.

For visitors, the effect is practical. US dollars are widely used, and many prices are quoted in dollars. Cards work in some places but often at unfavourable rates, and cash machines are unreliable. Bring cash, in small clean US dollar notes, and check how a place will charge you before you eat or check in.

Private generators supply most hotels and restaurants, so power cuts are seldom a problem for visitors, though air conditioning and lifts can be affected. The crisis has hit ordinary people hard, and many young Lebanese have left. It is also a reason that tourism income is valued.

The economy is also expensive for what it provides, because almost everything is imported and priced in dollars. Do not expect the low prices of neighbouring countries.

Worth knowing: Bring more cash than you expect to need, and keep it in several places. Check current information on ATM and card availability before you travel.

Moving around a small country with difficult roads

Lebanon's small size is deceptive. Beirut to Tripoli is 85 km but can take two hours in traffic, and mountain roads to the Cedars or the Chouf are steep and winding.

Drivers. A private driver is the standard way for visitors to see the country. It is comparatively expensive but gives you flexibility, local knowledge and a way of avoiding areas where conditions have changed.

Hire cars. They suit confident drivers who like independence. Traffic in Beirut is heavy and assertive, road signs are inconsistent, and roads can be poorly lit and unmarked.

Service taxis and buses. Cheap shared taxis and minibuses run on main routes, without timetables. They are useful for going between the main cities, less so for reaching the sites.

Checkpoints. The army operates checkpoints across the country. They are usually routine, but carry your passport and be polite.

Worth knowing: Do not drive at night in the mountains or in unfamiliar areas, and check current conditions in the south and the Bekaa with your hotel or driver before setting out.

When to go

Lebanon has four distinct seasons, and because of the range of altitudes you can ski and swim on the same day in the shoulder months.

April to June and September to November are the best months for sightseeing, the ruins and walking. The mountains are green in spring and golden in autumn, and the sea is warm from June to October.

Summer is hot and humid on the coast, but the mountains are cool, and it is the season for festivals and diaspora visitors. Prices peak in July and August.

Winter brings rain to the coast and snow to the mountains, and the ski season runs from December to March. The Cedars and the Qadisha Valley can be cut off after heavy snow.

Political and security conditions matter as much as the weather. Plan with flexibility, and keep bookings changeable for any part of the trip in the south or the Bekaa.

Culture, religion and etiquette

Lebanon's mix of communities makes it unusually tolerant in some ways and sensitive in others. Beirut and the coast are liberal and cosmopolitan, and the mountains and the north and south are more traditional.

Dress modestly in mosques, Tripoli and the south and the Bekaa, and take a scarf. Elsewhere, Western dress is normal. Ask before photographing people, and avoid photographing military or political sites.

Politics and history are sensitive. The civil war of 1975–1990, the Israeli wars and the current political system all divide people, and visitors are best to listen and ask questions.

Hospitality is a serious value. If you are offered coffee, sweets or a meal, accept graciously.

Three routes that work

  1. Four days: Beirut and the north coast

    Beirut for two nights, then Byblos and Batroun on a day trip, with Jeita Grotto on the way. It is compact and avoids the areas where advice is strictest.

    Tip Add a night in Batroun if you like beaches and food.

  2. One week: the north and the mountains

    Beirut, Byblos (Jbeil) and Batroun on the coast, Tripoli, then up to the Qadisha Valley and the Cedars, and back through the Chouf. A varied loop that stays away from the south and the Bekaa.

    Tip Hire a driver for the mountain days.

  3. Eight days: the full country

    The itinerary on the country page, adding Sidon (Saida), Tyre (Sour), Anjar & the Bekaa Wine Country and Baalbek if conditions and advice allow. It covers the Phoenician coast, the Roman temples and the Umayyad city.

    Tip Keep the south and the Bekaa days flexible, and be ready to swap them for more time in the mountains or on the coast.

FAQ

Planning a trip to Lebanon

Should I base myself in Beirut or move around?

Most visitors base themselves in Beirut for the first few nights and make day trips to Byblos, Jeita Grotto and the Chouf, then move to the north or the mountains for a night or two. Beirut's traffic makes long day trips tiring, so a change of base helps.

Do I need a driver?

For anything beyond Beirut, it is the easiest way. Lebanon has no railway and public transport is informal, and a driver knows the roads and current conditions. Hire cars work for confident drivers, but mountain roads and city traffic are demanding.

How does the security situation affect a trip?

It can change what is open and advisable at short notice. Advice is strictest for the south, the border areas of the Bekaa and Beirut's southern suburbs, and some governments advise against all travel. Read your government's current advice, buy flexible bookings and insurance, and be ready to skip areas.

Is it appropriate to visit while the country is struggling?

Many Lebanese hope so. Tourism supports guesthouses, restaurants, guides and wineries at a time when the economy has collapsed. The answer depends on the current advice, and on whether you can travel responsibly: spend locally, respect local customs and be ready to change your plans.
