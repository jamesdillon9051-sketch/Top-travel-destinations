Why go to Norway

Norway is genuinely defined by its coastline — a jagged, fjord-cut edge of Europe stretching further than the distance from London to Cairo, carved over ice ages into some of the most dramatic scenery on the continent.

That geography shapes everything about visiting: a single day's drive can pass waterfalls dropping straight into the sea, mountain villages reachable only by ferry, and roads that switchback up cliff faces to viewpoints that genuinely look unreal in photos until you're standing there yourself.

It's also a country of real extremes — the midnight sun keeps parts of the north in daylight for months each summer, while winter brings the northern lights and some of Europe's best conditions for seeing them, and Svalbard, far inside the Arctic Circle, holds more polar bears than people.

Experiences

Best things to do in Norway

The experiences that recur across the country and that make a Norway trip feel like more than a checklist of fjords.

  1. Cruise a fjord

    Genuinely the essential Norway experience, gliding between sheer rock walls and waterfalls on a boat cruise through one of the UNESCO-listed fjords.

    Tip A genuinely smaller boat or kayak tour gets far closer to the waterfalls than the larger cruise ships.

  2. See the northern lights

    Northern Norway is genuinely one of the most reliable places on Earth to see the aurora, visible on clear, dark nights roughly September to March.

    Tip Tromsø's genuinely aurora forecast and cloud cover map matter more than the activity index alone — clear skies are what counts.

  3. Hike to Preikestolen (Pulpit Rock)

    A genuinely spectacular flat rock plateau jutting 604 metres above Lysefjord, reached by a well-marked but genuinely strenuous day hike.

    Tip Start genuinely early to avoid both the midday crowds and afternoon cloud that can roll in.

  4. Drive the Atlantic Road

    A genuinely thrilling stretch of road hopping between small islands on low bridges, with the open Norwegian Sea crashing on either side.

    Tip Storm season genuinely brings dramatic waves crashing over the road — thrilling from a safe distance.

  5. Spot reindeer in the wild

    Genuinely common across northern Norway, where reindeer herding remains a living Sami tradition rather than a tourist display.

    Tip Drive genuinely carefully on rural northern roads — reindeer cross unpredictably, especially at dusk.

  6. Experience the midnight sun

    North of the Arctic Circle, the sun genuinely doesn't set at all for weeks around midsummer, turning midnight into golden-hour light.

    Tip Pack genuinely a proper eye mask — hotel blackout curtains alone often aren't enough for sleeping in constant daylight.

Food and drink

Norway foods to try

Norwegian food's reputation abroad rarely does justice to a genuinely serious seafood and dairy culture found at every price point.

  • Salmon (gravlax)

    Genuinely among the best in the world, whether cured as gravlax, smoked, or simply grilled fresh from the fjords.

    Where to try it Restaurants and fish markets nationwide, especially in Bergen.

  • Brunost (brown cheese)

    A genuinely uniquely Norwegian caramelised whey cheese, sweet and tangy, traditionally sliced thin with a special cheese slicer.

    Where to try it Breakfast tables and supermarkets nationwide — a genuine daily staple.

  • Fårikål

    Genuinely Norway's official national dish, a simple, hearty stew of lamb and cabbage, traditionally eaten in autumn.

    Where to try it Traditional restaurants nationwide, especially September through October.

  • Cloudberries (molter)

    A genuinely prized wild arctic berry, amber-coloured and tart-sweet, traditionally served with whipped cream as a dessert.

    Where to try it Restaurants across northern Norway in late summer, when the berries ripen.

  • Reindeer

    A genuinely traditional Sami food from the north, served as stew, smoked, or dried, with a rich, slightly gamey flavour.

    Where to try it Restaurants in Tromsø and other northern towns.

  • Vafler (waffles)

    Genuinely a beloved Norwegian snack, heart-shaped and softer than Belgian waffles, usually topped simply with sour cream and jam.

    Where to try it Cafés and mountain cabins nationwide.

When to go

Best time to visit Norway

Norway is genuinely a year-round destination, but summer and winter offer such different experiences that the season you choose shapes the whole trip.

  • Summer (June–August)

    Best time

    Near-endless daylight in the north, all mountain roads open, and the mildest, most reliable weather — also genuinely the busiest and most expensive season.

  • Autumn (September–October)

    Good

    Genuinely quieter, with the northern lights becoming visible again as nights darken, though weather turns noticeably less predictable.

  • Winter (November–February)

    Good

    Short days but genuinely dramatic, with the best northern lights odds and a real chance of snow closing mountain roads.

  • Spring (March–May)

    Good

    Increasingly long days and thinner crowds than summer, though some mountain roads genuinely stay closed until May or June.

Worth planning around: Attempting high mountain passes or the Atlantic Road outside May–September, when winter storms and snow genuinely make driving hazardous or impossible.

Trip length

How long to spend in Norway

Norway's fjords and its sheer north-south length make a full loop genuinely rewarding but time-hungry — rushing it defeats much of the point.

  • 3–4 days

    Oslo and Bergen, connected by the scenic Bergen Line train, the classic short Norway trip.

    Genuinely the most popular short itinerary, covering the country's two largest cities without a fjord-region commitment.

  • 5–7 days

    Oslo, Bergen and a fjord region such as Geirangerfjord or the Sognefjord area, connected by train, bus and ferry.

    Enough time to add real fjord depth to the classic city route.

  • 8–10 days

    The itinerary on this page: Oslo, Bergen, the fjords and Ålesund, at a genuinely unrushed pace.

    A genuinely complete first look at Norway's southern and western highlights.

  • 2 weeks or more

    Add Lofoten, Tromsø or Svalbard in full, all genuinely worth dedicated time rather than a rushed detour.

    The far north and Svalbard are where a second Norway trip belongs.

Getting around

Transportation in Norway

A mix of rental car, train and ferry is genuinely the standard way to see Norway — the fjord-cut geography makes any single mode of transport insufficient on its own.

  • Rental car

    Genuinely essential for reaching Geirangerfjord, Preikestolen and the Atlantic Road at a flexible pace.

    Winter tyres and real caution are genuinely required outside summer on mountain roads.

  • Domestic flights

    Connect Oslo to Tromsø, Svalbard and other distant regions in under two hours, essential given Norway's length.

    Widely used even by Norwegians themselves, given the country's sheer north-south distance.

  • The Bergen Line and other scenic trains

    A genuinely spectacular rail route connecting Oslo and Bergen over the mountains.

    Widely considered one of the world's great train journeys in its own right.

  • Hurtigruten coastal ferry

    A genuinely iconic working ferry route up the entire coast, stopping at towns including Trondheim and Tromsø.

    Both a practical transport option and a popular cruise-style holiday in itself.

  • Local ferries and express boats

    Genuinely essential for reaching fjordside villages and islands with no road access.

    Many are included in Norway's otherwise excellent public transport ticketing.

Culture

Culture and etiquette in Norway

Norwegian culture is shaped by a genuine closeness to nature (friluftsliv, or 'open-air living'), a strong sense of equality, and real pride in the country's fishing, seafaring and Sami heritage.

  • Shoes off indoors

    Removing shoes at the door is genuinely standard in Norwegian homes, even for brief visits.

  • Norwegians are genuinely reserved with strangers, warm with friends

    Small talk with strangers is less common than in some cultures — this is reserve, not unfriendliness.

  • Respect the outdoors and 'allemannsretten'

    Norway's genuinely generous 'right to roam' law comes with real responsibility to leave no trace.

  • Nature's risks are taken seriously here

    Sudden weather changes, avalanche terrain and tidal fjord currents are genuinely responsible for most visitor incidents — heed posted warnings.

  • Tipping is genuinely modest

    Rounding up or a small tip for excellent service is welcome, but nothing like the norms in the US.

  • Book ahead in summer, without exception

    Accommodation and popular hikes genuinely sell out months ahead across the peak June–August season.

Do

  • Take off your shoes when entering a home.
  • Book accommodation and any fjord cruise well ahead for a summer trip.
  • Check road conditions and ferry schedules before any fjord-region drive.
  • Bring layers for every season — mountain and fjord weather genuinely changes fast.

Don't

  • Don't leave litter on hiking trails — Norway's 'right to roam' comes with real responsibility.
  • Don't ignore posted warnings at cliffs, waterfalls and fjord viewpoints — sudden weather is a genuine, serious hazard.
  • Don't expect to tip heavily — it's modest and never obligatory.
  • Don't attempt high mountain roads or hikes without checking current conditions and closures.

Travel tips

Norway travel tips

The things that most often go wrong on a Norway trip, in rough order of how much they cost people.

  • Book your rental car, accommodation and any fjord cruise well ahead for summer travel.
  • Check road and ferry conditions before any fjord-region drive, especially outside summer.
  • Budget for genuinely high prices — Norway is one of the most expensive countries in Europe to visit.
  • Pack proper layers and a real waterproof shell regardless of season.
  • Never approach cliff edges at Preikestolen or similar viewpoints without care — falls are genuinely dangerous.
  • Consider Svalbard only as a dedicated add-on with a guided operator — independent travel there is genuinely restricted for safety.
  • Fill up on fuel whenever you see a station in rural fjord areas — distances between them are genuinely longer than they look.
  • Buy alcohol at the duty-free shop on arrival — it's genuinely cheaper than at Norway's state-run Vinmonopolet stores.

FAQ

Visiting Norway: frequently asked questions

How many days do you need in Norway?

A week to ten days covers Oslo, Bergen and the western fjords comfortably, which is the itinerary on this page. Three to four days is enough for Oslo and Bergen alone, and genuinely still worthwhile.

What is the best time of year to visit Norway?

June to August for long daylight and full access to mountain roads and fjord cruises; September to March for a good chance at the northern lights in the north.

Do you need a car in Norway?

For the fjord regions, genuinely yes — a rental car or a mix of train, bus and ferry is the standard way most visitors see the country beyond its cities.

Is Norway expensive?

Yes, genuinely one of the most expensive countries in Europe — mid-range travel runs about NOK 1,500–2,500 per person per day. Supermarket meals and a hostel or guesthouse keep costs down.

Can you see the northern lights in Norway?

Yes, genuinely one of the best places in the world for it, roughly September to March in the north on clear, dark nights — never guaranteed, but a real, frequent possibility above the Arctic Circle.

Is Norway safe to visit?

Extremely safe from a crime perspective, consistently ranked among the world's most peaceful countries. The real risks are environmental — fjord currents, avalanche terrain and fast-changing mountain weather — treated with genuine respect by visitors and locals alike.
