How to plan a Norway trip: whether to focus on the cities, the fjords or the far north, when to go for the midnight sun versus the northern lights, what it costs, and a practical eight-day itinerary.

How Norway divides

Norway genuinely divides into a long chain of very different regions strung along its coastline — treating the whole country as one short loop is the most common planning mistake.

The southeast. Oslo, the capital, and its surrounding region — museums, a compact walkable centre, and the easiest part of the country to reach.

The fjord country. Bergen, Geirangerfjord and Ålesund and the Atlantic Road — Norway's most iconic scenery, and genuinely the reason most people visit.

The far north. Lofoten, Tromsø and Trondheim — the midnight sun, the northern lights, and dramatic Arctic island scenery.

The wild edges. Jotunheimen's high mountains and Svalbard's polar wilderness are both genuinely spectacular but reward visitors with more time and a bigger budget.

Worth knowing: Most first-time visitors genuinely combine Oslo, Bergen and one fjord region in a single trip. Svalbard and the far north are where a longer or second trip belongs.

Choosing between the ten destinations

  • If this is your first trip

    Oslo and Bergen, connected by the Bergen Line train, the classic short Norway route and genuinely the most popular for a reason.

  • If you want the classic fjord scenery

    Add Geirangerfjord and Ålesund to the Oslo-Bergen route for a genuinely complete first look at the fjords.

  • If you want the midnight sun and dramatic islands

    Lofoten, genuinely one of the most photogenic places in the entire country.

  • If you want the northern lights

    Tromsø, genuinely one of the most reliable and accessible places on Earth to see the aurora.

  • If you want serious mountain hiking

    Jotunheimen, home to Norway's highest peaks and genuinely excellent multi-day trekking.

  • If you want true Arctic wilderness

    Svalbard, genuinely still home to more polar bears than people, reachable only by guided tour.

Getting around: trains, ferries, and one common mistake

Norway's fjord-cut geography genuinely means no single mode of transport covers the whole country — a mix of train, ferry, bus and sometimes a domestic flight is standard.

Rent a car for the fjord regions. It's genuinely the most flexible way to see Geirangerfjord, Preikestolen and the Atlantic Road at your own pace.

Don't underestimate driving times. A route that looks like two hours on a map can genuinely take four with ferry waits and mountain switchbacks.

Take the Bergen Line at least once. The Oslo-Bergen train is genuinely one of the world's great scenic rail journeys, not just a way to get somewhere.

Fly for the far north. A domestic flight to Tromsø or Svalbard genuinely saves days of travel given Norway's sheer length.

Book ferries and popular hikes ahead in summer. Fjord ferries and the Preikestolen car park genuinely fill up fast in peak season.

Worth knowing: Book your rental car and accommodation months ahead for a June–August trip — Norway's fjord-region capacity genuinely sells out early in peak season.

When to go

Norway's seasons offer such different experiences that 'the best time' depends entirely on what you want to see. Two windows generally work best: June to August and September to March for the far north.

Summer brings near-endless daylight in the north, all mountain roads open, and the mildest weather — also genuinely the busiest and most expensive season.

Fjord regions are genuinely best visited May to September, when mountain passes and ferry schedules run at full capacity.

The northern lights season, roughly September to March, offers a genuinely good chance of the aurora above the Arctic Circle on clear, dark nights.

Deep winter is genuinely dramatic in the north — snow, the aurora and a real sense of the Arctic — but short days and road closures limit how much of the country you can cover.

Worth knowing: Book fjord-region accommodation and any Preikestolen or Trolltunga hike logistics many months ahead for June–August — this is genuinely the busiest and most tightly booked window of the year.

Four routes that work

  1. Four days: Oslo and Bergen

    Oslo and Bergen, connected by the scenic Bergen Line, genuinely the most popular short Norway itinerary.

    Tip Book a window seat on the Bergen Line and sit on the left travelling from Oslo for the best mountain views.

  2. Eight days: the classic fjord route

    The itinerary on the Norway country page: Oslo, Bergen, Geirangerfjord and Ålesund. A genuinely complete first look at Norway's southern and western highlights.

    Tip Break the fjord drive into realistic daily distances — Norway's roads reward a slower pace than the map alone suggests.

  3. A week focused on the far north

    Tromsø and Lofoten as a self-contained trip, genuinely distinct enough from the southern fjords to justify dedicated time on their own.

    Tip Visit in winter for the northern lights or summer for the midnight sun — the two seasons are almost entirely different trips.

  4. A Svalbard add-on for a second trip

    Svalbard by guided expedition, genuinely one of the most remote and otherworldly landscapes in a country full of them.

    Tip Book a genuinely reputable guided operator — independent travel outside Longyearbyen is restricted for real safety reasons.

FAQ

Planning a trip to Norway

How many days do you need in Norway?

A week to ten days covers Oslo, Bergen and the western fjords at a reasonable pace, which is the itinerary in this guide. Three to four days is enough for Oslo and Bergen, and genuinely still worthwhile on its own.

Is the fjord scenery worth the cost of visiting Norway?

Yes, genuinely — the fjords are a large part of why people come, and no photograph quite captures standing beneath cliffs that rise straight out of the water.

Do you need a car in Norway?

For the fjord regions, yes, genuinely — though a combination of trains, buses and ferries also covers the country well without one.

When should I visit to see the northern lights?

Roughly September to March in northern Norway, on clear, dark nights — genuinely never guaranteed, but a real, frequent possibility above the Arctic Circle.
