Why go to Austria

Austria packs an outsized cultural legacy into a country smaller than Maine — Vienna alone produced or hosted Mozart, Beethoven, Freud and Klimt, and its imperial Habsburg architecture still defines what most people picture when they imagine a European capital. That legacy is genuinely lived-in rather than museum-cased, from the Vienna Philharmonic's regular performances to coffee houses still run the way they were a century ago.

Beyond the cities, Austria is genuinely one of the most consistently beautiful countries in Europe to simply look at. The Alps run the length of the country, and the combination of turquoise lakes, church-topped villages and snow-capped peaks around the Salzkammergut and Tyrol regions produces scenery that looks retouched even when it isn't.

The practical spine connecting all of this is a genuinely excellent rail network — Austria's trains are punctual, comfortable and reach even small Alpine villages — alongside a food and coffee house culture, and a directness in manner that first-time visitors sometimes mistake for coldness before realizing it's simply Austrian efficiency.

Experiences

Best things to do in Austria

The experiences that recur across the country and that make an Austria trip feel like more than a checklist of palaces and peaks.

  1. Sit in a proper Viennese coffee house

    A genuine Austrian institution, UNESCO-recognised even, where a single coffee buys the right to sit for hours with a newspaper and no one will rush you.

    Tip Order a Melange, Vienna's classic milk coffee, and expect a glass of water served alongside it without asking.

  2. Ski or hike in the Alps

    Austria's Alpine regions genuinely rival Switzerland and France for skiing in winter and hiking in summer, often at a noticeably lower cost.

    Tip Summer hiking is considerably cheaper than winter skiing and avoids the peak-season crowds and prices entirely.

  3. Attend a classical music performance

    Vienna's musical heritage is genuinely still active, not just historical, with regular performances by the Vienna Philharmonic and countless smaller ensembles across the city.

    Tip Standing-room tickets at the Vienna State Opera are genuinely affordable and sold only on the day, a real Vienna institution worth queuing for.

  4. Explore an Alpine lake

    The Salzkammergut region alone holds dozens of genuinely postcard-worthy Alpine lakes, several swimmable in summer and stunning year-round.

    Tip Hallstättersee and Wolfgangsee are the most visited; Grundlsee and Altaussee offer genuinely comparable beauty with noticeably fewer crowds.

  5. Try Austrian wine in the Wachau Valley

    A genuinely underrated wine region along the Danube, its steep terraced vineyards producing excellent Grüner Veltliner and Riesling rarely exported widely.

    Tip A river cruise or cycle path along the Danube is a genuinely relaxed way to combine several Wachau wine villages in one day.

  6. Eat cake properly, with cream

    Austrian cake culture is genuinely serious business, from Sachertorte's chocolate density to the endless variety found in any proper konditorei.

    Tip Always accept the whipped cream (Schlagobers) offered alongside — it's the traditional accompaniment, not an optional extra.

Food and drink

Austria foods to try

Austrian food's reputation for schnitzel and strudel undersells a genuinely rich, hearty cuisine shaped by the old Habsburg empire's many regions.

  • Wiener schnitzel

    A thin, breaded and fried veal cutlet, Austria's most famous dish and genuinely a point of national pride over how it should be properly prepared.

    Where to try it Traditional gasthauses nationwide — a genuine veal version, not the cheaper pork often substituted, is worth seeking out.

  • Sachertorte

    A dense chocolate cake with a thin layer of apricot jam, genuinely the subject of a decades-long legal dispute over which Vienna hotel owns the 'original' recipe.

    Where to try it Hotel Sacher in Vienna for the original, or Demel, its longtime rival, for a genuinely comparable version.

  • Tafelspitz

    Boiled beef served with root vegetables, apple-horseradish and chive sauce, genuinely a Habsburg-era classic said to have been Emperor Franz Joseph's favourite dish.

    Where to try it Traditional Viennese restaurants, several claiming a direct historical link to the imperial court kitchen.

  • Kaiserschmarrn

    A shredded, caramelised pancake dusted with sugar and served with fruit compote, genuinely more dessert than breakfast despite its humble origins.

    Where to try it Alpine mountain huts and traditional restaurants nationwide, particularly satisfying after a day of hiking or skiing.

  • Austrian wine

    A genuinely excellent, underrated wine-producing country, particularly for crisp whites like Grüner Veltliner from the Wachau and Kamptal regions.

    Where to try it Wachau Valley wineries and Viennese heurigen, traditional wine taverns serving the current vintage alongside simple food.

  • Apfelstrudel

    Thin pastry rolled around spiced apple filling, genuinely one of the defining desserts of Austrian and wider Central European cuisine.

    Where to try it Konditoreis and traditional restaurants nationwide, best served warm with vanilla sauce or cream.

When to go

Best time to visit Austria

Austria genuinely has two best seasons depending on your focus: late spring and early autumn for the cities, and winter for the Alpine ski regions.

  • Spring (April–June)

    Best time

    Mild, increasingly warm weather ideal for exploring Vienna, Salzburg and Alpine hiking trails as the snow clears.

  • Summer (July–August)

    Good

    Warm and reliable for lake swimming and hiking, though genuinely the most crowded and expensive months in the popular Salzkammergut region.

  • Autumn (September–October)

    Best time

    Comfortable temperatures, thinning crowds and the wine harvest in full swing across the Wachau Valley.

  • Winter (November–March)

    Good

    Cold but genuinely magical for Vienna's Christmas markets and essential for Alpine skiing, with December through February the ski season's core.

Worth planning around: August without accommodation booked ahead in the Salzkammergut lake district — Austrian and German domestic summer holidays push the region to its busiest, most expensive point of the year.

Trip length

How long to spend in Austria

Austria's compact size and excellent train network make an ambitious city-and-Alps itinerary genuinely workable in under two weeks.

  • 4–5 days

    Vienna alone fills four or five days, or Vienna plus a day trip to the Wachau Valley or Salzburg by fast train.

    A short Austria trip is better spent deeply in Vienna than spread thin across the country.

  • 8–10 days

    Vienna, then Salzburg and Hallstatt — the classic first-trip route connecting imperial history and Alpine scenery.

    Add a day in the Wachau Valley for a genuinely well-rounded route without extending the core itinerary much.

  • 12–16 days

    The itinerary on this page: Vienna, the Wachau Valley, Salzburg, Hallstatt, then Innsbruck and the Alps, covering both imperial cities and mountain scenery.

    Enough time to add Graz or the Grossglockner High Alpine Road to the classic Vienna-and-Alps route.

  • 3 weeks or more

    Add Graz, Bregenz on Lake Constance, or a slower, unhurried pace through the Salzkammergut's smaller lake villages.

    The quieter western regions and a genuinely unhurried Alpine pace are where a second Austria trip belongs.

Getting around

Transportation in Austria

Austria's ÖBB rail network is genuinely one of Europe's best, connecting cities and even small Alpine towns reliably.

  • Intercity trains (ÖBB Railjet)

    Fast, comfortable trains connect Vienna, Salzburg, Graz and Innsbruck efficiently.

    Book ahead for discounted 'Sparschiene' fares, or use a rail pass for extensive multi-city travel.

  • Regional trains and postbuses

    Reach smaller destinations including Hallstatt, the Wachau Valley and Kitzbühel reliably from the main rail network.

    Regional day tickets often work out cheaper than individual fares for multi-stop Alpine village trips.

  • Rental car

    Useful for the Grossglockner High Alpine Road and exploring smaller Alpine villages at a flexible pace beyond the rail network.

    Winter driving in the Alps requires genuine experience with snow chains and conditions — consider a guided option instead.

  • Danube river cruise or cycle path

    A scenic alternative for exploring the Wachau Valley's wine villages between Vienna and Melk.

    The Danube cycle path is genuinely one of Europe's best-maintained long-distance routes, well signed and largely flat.

  • Vienna's U-Bahn and trams

    A clean, efficient system covering central Vienna well, including direct connections to the airport.

    A 24, 48 or 72-hour Vienna City Card covers unlimited transit alongside museum discounts.

Culture

Culture and etiquette in Austria

Austrian culture is shaped by its imperial history and Alpine geography alike, with a directness in manner and a strong respect for order and punctuality holding nationwide.

  • Titles and formality still matter

    Austrians often use formal address (Sie) and professional titles more than many visitors expect — let a local lead the shift to first names.

  • Punctuality is genuinely taken seriously

    Arriving on time, whether for a train or a dinner reservation, is expected rather than optional.

  • Directness isn't rudeness

    Austrians can come across as blunt to visitors used to more indirect cultures — it's typically efficiency, not unfriendliness.

  • Sunday is genuinely a day of rest

    Most shops close on Sundays and public holidays nationwide — plan grocery and errand needs accordingly.

  • Jaywalking is frowned upon

    Austrians overwhelmingly wait for the pedestrian light even with no traffic in sight — a small but genuine local norm.

  • Coffee house etiquette is real

    Ordering a single coffee genuinely does buy you the right to sit for hours, but flag down your server rather than expecting them to hover.

Do

  • Greet with a firm handshake and formal address until invited to switch to first names
  • Arrive on time for reservations, tours and train departures
  • Wait for pedestrian lights even when the street looks clear
  • Plan grocery shopping around Sunday closures
  • Book Alpine hotels and Salzkammergut accommodation well ahead for summer and ski season
  • Carry some cash for smaller towns and traditional restaurants

Don't

  • Don't jaywalk — it's a genuinely visible local norm, not just a rule
  • Don't assume every restaurant or shop takes cards in smaller towns
  • Don't rush a coffee house visit — lingering is the entire point
  • Don't be surprised by directness in customer service — it isn't meant unkindly
  • Don't expect shops open on Sundays outside train stations and tourist areas
  • Don't drive Alpine mountain passes in winter without genuine snow driving experience

Travel tips

Austria travel tips

The things that most often go wrong on an Austria trip, in rough order of how much they cost people.

  • Book Alpine and Salzkammergut lake accommodation well ahead for summer and peak ski season.
  • Plan grocery and errand shopping around Sunday closures, which apply nationwide.
  • Carry some cash for smaller towns, market stalls and traditional restaurants.
  • Book Vienna State Opera standing-room tickets on the day rather than assuming advance booking is possible.
  • Reserve Schönbrunn Palace and Hallstatt salt mine tickets online ahead in summer, when queues can be long.
  • Pack proper layers for Alpine regions, where temperatures can swing considerably even in summer.
  • Avoid winter mountain driving without genuine experience — consider trains or a guided tour instead.
  • Validate train tickets where required on regional lines — Austria's system relies on honesty checks.

FAQ

Visiting Austria: frequently asked questions

How many days do you need in Austria?

Ten to fourteen for a first trip. Eight to ten covers Vienna and either Salzburg or the Alps with reasonable pacing; twelve to sixteen lets you add Innsbruck and the Alps to the classic Vienna-and-Salzburg route, which is the itinerary on this page. Four or five days is enough for Vienna alone, and Vienna alone genuinely justifies a trip.

What is the best time of year to visit Austria?

May to June and September are the sweet spots for cities and hiking — warm weather, manageable crowds and lower prices than peak summer. December to March is essential if skiing matters, and Vienna's Christmas markets make winter genuinely worthwhile even without skiing.

Do I need a car in Austria?

Not for the cities or main tourist route — the train network covers Vienna, Salzburg, Hallstatt, Innsbruck and Graz excellently. A car becomes genuinely useful for the Grossglockner High Alpine Road and reaching smaller Alpine villages off the rail network.

Is Austria expensive?

Mid-range travel runs about €110–180 per person per day, with Vienna and peak ski season adding a genuine premium over that figure. Booking trains ahead and eating at traditional gasthauses rather than tourist restaurants are the biggest levers on cost.

Is Austria safe for tourists?

Very. Violent crime against visitors is rare, and most areas are safe to walk in the evening. Standard vigilance around bags on Vienna's public transport is the main practical precaution.

Should I visit Vienna or the Alps first?

Vienna, for almost everyone — it's the main international gateway and the Wachau Valley is an easy day trip from there. Salzburg and Hallstatt then work naturally as the bridge between the capital and the deeper Alpine regions like Innsbruck.
