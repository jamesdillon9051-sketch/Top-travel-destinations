Why go to Portugal

Portugal has spent the last decade quietly becoming one of Europe's most talked-about destinations, and the case for it holds up in person: Lisbon and Porto both pair genuine hillside charm — tiled facades, steep funiculars, pastel houses stacked above a river — with a cost of living noticeably lower than Spain, France or Italy for a genuinely comparable quality of food, wine and history.

That value extends well beyond the two big cities. The Douro Valley's terraced vineyards have grown wine for over two thousand years, port wine among them since the 17th century, Sintra's hillside palaces look like they were designed for a fairy tale rather than a Portuguese king's actual summer retreat, and the Algarve's dramatic limestone cliffs meet Atlantic water that stays genuinely swimmable without the Mediterranean's summer crowds.

Portugal's islands push the range further still — Madeira's volcanic peaks and levada trails, and the Azores' genuinely wild, still-active volcanic landscape, are both a short flight from the mainland yet feel like an entirely different country. A well-developed train network between the mainland's cities, plus the islands' own domestic flight connections, make combining several of these in one trip genuinely straightforward.

Experiences

Best things to do in Portugal

The experiences that recur across the country and that make a Portugal trip feel like more than a checklist of tiled facades and pastéis de nata.

  1. Ride a historic tram uphill

    Lisbon's rattling yellow trams, particularly Tram 28, climb genuinely steep hills through the old city, a functioning transit system as much as a tourist ride.

    Tip Ride Tram 28 early morning or late in the day, when it's noticeably less packed than the middle of the day.

  2. Eat a proper pastel de nata

    Portugal's beloved custard tart, genuinely best eaten warm with a dusting of cinnamon, is a national institution rather than just a tourist souvenir.

    Tip Pastéis de Belém in Lisbon bakes to the original 19th-century recipe, though genuinely good versions exist at bakeries nationwide.

  3. Listen to fado

    Portugal's melancholic traditional music, genuinely rooted in Lisbon's working-class neighbourhoods, is best experienced at a small, unamplified venue rather than a tourist dinner-show.

    Tip Seek out a genuine casa de fado in Alfama or Mouraria over a scripted tourist-menu version elsewhere in the city.

  4. Taste port wine at its source

    Port wine's genuine home is the Douro Valley's steep terraced vineyards, though the wine's cellars and tastings are concentrated across the river from Porto in Vila Nova de Gaia.

    Tip A day trip into the Douro Valley itself, not just a Porto cellar tour, gives a genuinely fuller picture of where port actually comes from.

  5. Walk a genuinely dramatic Atlantic coastline

    From the Algarve's limestone cliffs to the wilder beaches further north, Portugal's Atlantic coast offers genuinely striking scenery without the Mediterranean's summer crowds.

    Tip The Algarve's less-developed western coast (Costa Vicentina) offers genuinely dramatic cliffs with noticeably fewer visitors than the eastern resort strip.

  6. Explore a hilltop castle or palace

    Sintra's palaces and countless hilltop castles nationwide reflect Portugal's genuinely deep royal and Moorish architectural history.

    Tip Arrive at opening time for Sintra's most famous palaces, which fill with day-trip crowds from Lisbon by mid-morning.

Food and drink

Portugal foods to try

Portuguese food's reputation abroad rarely does justice to a genuinely serious seafood and regional wine culture found at every price point.

  • Bacalhau (salt cod)

    Portugal's national ingredient, genuinely said to have a different recipe for every day of the year, from bacalhau à brás to grilled whole fillets.

    Where to try it Traditional tascas nationwide — a genuinely good bacalhau dish is a reliable measure of a restaurant's quality.

  • Pastéis de nata

    Flaky pastry custard tarts, genuinely one of Portugal's most beloved and widely exported culinary exports, best eaten fresh and warm.

    Where to try it Pastéis de Belém in Lisbon for the original recipe, or any genuine local pastelaria nationwide.

  • Francesinha

    Porto's genuinely indulgent sandwich, layered with meats, melted cheese and a spicy beer-and-tomato sauce, a defining northern Portuguese comfort food.

    Where to try it Traditional restaurants in Porto, where the dish genuinely originated and remains a point of local pride.

  • Grilled sardines

    Simply grilled whole sardines, genuinely a summer staple especially during Lisbon's Santo António festival in June.

    Where to try it Street stalls and traditional restaurants nationwide, particularly excellent in coastal towns.

  • Port wine

    A genuinely globally recognised fortified wine, produced exclusively in the Douro Valley, with styles ranging from ruby to aged tawny.

    Where to try it Port lodges in Vila Nova de Gaia across from Porto, or wineries directly in the Douro Valley.

  • Caldo verde

    A genuinely simple, comforting kale and potato soup with slices of chouriço, a defining Portuguese home-cooking classic.

    Where to try it Traditional restaurants nationwide, especially welcome in cooler months.

When to go

Best time to visit Portugal

Portugal's mainland summer heat and crowds peak in July and August, and spring and early autumn offer the best balance of warm weather, open facilities and manageable crowds.

  • Spring (March–May)

    Best time

    Mild, increasingly warm weather with wildflowers across the countryside and crowds well below the July-August peak.

  • Summer (June–August)

    Good

    Hot and dry, especially inland, and the most reliable weather for the Algarve's beaches, but also genuinely the most crowded and expensive months.

  • Autumn (September–October)

    Best time

    Warm sea temperatures lingering from summer, comfortable air temperatures and noticeably thinner crowds than August.

  • Winter (November–February)

    Shoulder

    Mild by northern European standards on the mainland, wetter in the north, with the islands staying genuinely temperate year-round.

Worth planning around: The Algarve in August without accommodation booked far ahead — Portuguese and European summer holidays push the region to its busiest, most expensive point of the year.

Trip length

How long to spend in Portugal

Portugal's compact mainland and good train network make an ambitious city-and-coast itinerary genuinely workable, though the islands each reward dedicated time of their own.

  • 4–5 days

    Lisbon alone fills four or five days, or Lisbon plus a day trip to Sintra.

    A short Portugal trip is better spent deeply in Lisbon than spread thin across the country.

  • 8–10 days

    Lisbon, Sintra and Porto, connected by train — the classic first-trip route connecting Portugal's two great cities.

    Add a day in Óbidos or the Douro Valley for a genuinely well-rounded route without extending the core itinerary much.

  • 12–16 days

    The itinerary on this page: Lisbon, Sintra, the Douro Valley, Porto, then south to Évora and the Algarve, covering both the north's wine country and the south's coastline.

    Enough time to add Coimbra or Óbidos to the classic Lisbon-and-Porto route.

  • 3 weeks or more

    Add Madeira or the Azores, both genuinely worth a dedicated 4–5 day stay rather than a rushed add-on.

    The islands and a genuinely unhurried pace through the Alentejo's inland towns are where a second Portugal trip belongs.

Getting around

Transportation in Portugal

Portugal's train network connects the mainland's main cities well, while a rental car opens up the Algarve, Douro Valley and Alentejo.

  • Intercity trains

    Fast, comfortable trains connect Lisbon, Porto and Coimbra efficiently.

    Book Alfa Pendular high-speed fares ahead for discounted prices, particularly for the Lisbon-Porto route.

  • Regional trains

    Reach Sintra and Óbidos reliably from Lisbon, both easy day trips without a car.

    Sintra's train runs frequently from Lisbon's Rossio station, taking about 40 minutes.

  • Rental car

    Genuinely essential for exploring the Douro Valley's wineries, the wider Algarve coastline and Évora's Alentejo countryside at a flexible pace.

    An International Driving Permit is recommended, and central Lisbon and Porto are best explored without a car.

  • Domestic flights to the islands

    Connect the mainland to Madeira and the Azores in under two hours.

    Book ahead for the best fares, particularly in summer when island demand peaks.

  • Lisbon's trams, metro and ferries

    A characterful, if occasionally slow, network covering central Lisbon well, including the iconic hill-climbing trams.

    A Viva Viagem card covers metro, bus, tram and ferry within the same fare system.

Culture

Culture and etiquette in Portugal

Portuguese culture is shaped by a genuine maritime history and a warm, unhurried hospitality, alongside real pride in local food, wine and football.

  • Meals run unhurried and social

    Lunch and dinner are genuinely social occasions in Portugal, rarely rushed, and service reflects that relaxed pace rather than inattentiveness.

  • A greeting matters

    A polite 'bom dia' or 'boa tarde' when entering a shop or restaurant is genuinely appreciated and expected.

  • Bread and olives aren't automatically free

    Table snacks brought unprompted (couvert) are usually charged if you eat them — it's fine to decline them politely if you'd rather not pay.

  • Football is genuinely a national passion

    Conversations about Portuguese football, particularly the big three clubs, are a genuine social icebreaker nationwide.

  • Punctuality is relaxed but expected for reservations

    Social plans run flexibly, but restaurant reservations and tours are still expected to be honoured on time.

  • August is a genuine national holiday period

    Many smaller businesses close for part of August as owners take their own holidays, particularly outside the main tourist cities.

Do

  • Greet shopkeepers and restaurant staff with a polite hello
  • Try the local wine wherever you are — regional variety is genuinely significant
  • Linger over meals rather than rushing — it's part of the social occasion
  • Learn a few basic Portuguese phrases — the effort is genuinely appreciated
  • Book Algarve and island accommodation well ahead for July and August travel
  • Wear comfortable shoes — cobblestone streets are steep and genuinely slippery when worn or wet

Don't

  • Don't assume free bread and olives at a restaurant table — you'll likely be charged if you eat them
  • Don't rush a meal — Portuguese dining is deliberately unhurried
  • Don't be surprised if some smaller businesses close for part of August
  • Don't wear flip-flops on Lisbon or Porto's steep cobblestone hills — proper shoes matter
  • Don't skip a reservation confirmation for popular restaurants in peak season
  • Don't assume every small-town business takes cards — carry some cash as backup

Travel tips

Portugal travel tips

The things that most often go wrong on a Portugal trip, in rough order of how much they cost people.

  • Book Algarve and island accommodation as early as possible for July and August travel.
  • Wear proper shoes for Lisbon and Porto's steep, often slippery cobblestone streets.
  • Rent a car for the Douro Valley, Algarve and Alentejo — public transport is limited outside the main cities.
  • Carry some cash for smaller towns and markets, though card acceptance is generally strong.
  • Book Sintra's palaces online ahead in summer, when queues can mean significant waiting.
  • Check whether table snacks (couvert) are charged before eating them at a restaurant.
  • Pack for real heat inland and in the Algarve in July and August, and plan around the midday sun.
  • Book domestic flights to Madeira or the Azores ahead, particularly in summer when island demand peaks.

FAQ

Visiting Portugal: frequently asked questions

How many days do you need in Portugal?

Ten to fourteen for a first trip. Eight to ten covers Lisbon, Sintra and Porto with reasonable pacing; twelve to sixteen lets you add the Douro Valley, Évora and the Algarve, which is the itinerary on this page. Four or five days is enough for Lisbon alone, and Lisbon alone genuinely justifies a trip.

What is the best time of year to visit Portugal?

April to June and September to October are the sweet spots — warm weather, manageable crowds and lower prices than peak summer. July and August bring the most reliable Algarve beach weather at the cost of genuine crowds and heat.

Do I need a car in Portugal?

Not for Lisbon and Porto, both well covered by trains and trams, and Sintra and Óbidos are easy train day trips. A car becomes genuinely useful for the Douro Valley, the wider Algarve coast and the Alentejo's countryside.

Is Portugal expensive?

Mid-range travel runs about €85–140 per person per day, noticeably lower than Spain, France or Italy for comparable quality. Booking the Algarve and islands well ahead and eating at neighbourhood tascas rather than tourist restaurants are the biggest levers on cost.

Is Portugal safe for tourists?

Very — Portugal consistently ranks among Europe's safest countries. Pickpocketing on Lisbon's crowded trams and in tourist areas is the main practical risk, manageable with ordinary vigilance.

Should I visit Madeira or the Azores?

Both are genuinely worthwhile but distinct — Madeira offers dramatic volcanic peaks and levada hiking trails with well-developed tourism infrastructure, while the Azores feel wilder and less developed, with active volcanic landscapes and genuinely fewer visitors.
