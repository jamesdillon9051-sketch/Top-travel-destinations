Welcome to Ayutthaya

Ayutthaya was the capital of the Siamese kingdom for over 400 years, from 1350 until Burmese forces destroyed it in 1767, and at its height was among the largest and wealthiest cities in the world, a major trading hub visited by European, Japanese, Chinese and Persian merchants alike.

What remains today is a genuinely evocative field of brick and stone ruins — rows of headless Buddha statues, towering prang spires and one famously serene sandstone Buddha head grown into a fig tree's roots — spread across an island formed by three converging rivers, now a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Ayutthaya's proximity to Bangkok, under two hours by train or road, makes it one of the most straightforward genuinely significant historical sites to add to a Thailand itinerary, whether as a day trip or an overnight stay away from the capital's crowds.

Why visit

Why visit Ayutthaya?

Ayutthaya's case is that no other easy Bangkok day trip delivers this much genuine ancient history in one place.

  • The ruins are genuinely evocative rather than roped-off museum pieces

    Rows of headless Buddha statues and crumbling prang spires can be explored up close, mostly on foot or by bicycle.

  • The tree-root Buddha head is a genuinely singular sight

    Wat Mahathat's sandstone Buddha head, cradled within fig tree roots, is unlike anything else in Thailand.

  • It's a UNESCO World Heritage Site for real reasons

    The historical park's scale and preservation genuinely justify the listing, not just the name recognition.

  • It's a genuinely easy addition to a Bangkok-based trip

    Under two hours away by train, making a full, worthwhile day trip without extending your itinerary much.

  • An overnight stay reveals the ruins lit at dusk without the crowds

    Day-trippers leave by late afternoon, leaving the temples genuinely quiet for an evening visit.

Main attractions

Top things to see in Ayutthaya

Ayutthaya's essential ruins, spread across the historical park at the heart of the old city island.

  1. 01

    Wat Mahathat

    Home to Ayutthaya's most famous sight, a sandstone Buddha head grown into a fig tree's roots, alongside rows of headless Buddha statues and a crumbling central prang.

    Tip Never pose for a photo taller than the Buddha head — kneeling to take the picture is both respectful and required by park rules.

  2. 02

    Wat Chaiwatthanaram

    A genuinely striking riverside temple built in Khmer Angkor style, its central prang surrounded by smaller towers, best appreciated from across the river at sunset.

    Tip Visit for sunset from the riverbank opposite for the temple's silhouette reflected in the water.

  3. 03

    Wat Phra Si Sanphet

    The former royal temple within the old palace grounds, its three iconic bell-shaped chedis genuinely among the most photographed structures in Ayutthaya.

    Tip Visit early morning for the best light on the three chedis and considerably fewer visitors than midday.

  4. 04

    Wat Ratchaburana

    A well-preserved central prang that visitors can climb inside, its steep, narrow staircase leading to a chamber that once held royal ashes and treasure.

    Tip The climb inside is steep and low-ceilinged — not recommended if you're uncomfortable in confined spaces.

  5. 05

    Wat Lokayasutharam

    Home to a 37-metre reclining Buddha statue set in an open field, a genuinely peaceful and less crowded stop than the park's central temples.

    Tip The open setting makes this a genuinely good sunset spot with far fewer visitors than the main historical park.

  6. 06

    The Ayutthaya Historical Study Centre

    A genuinely worthwhile museum providing context on the city's history as a major international trading port, useful either before or after exploring the ruins themselves.

    Tip Visit before the temples if you can — the context genuinely deepens appreciation of what you're about to see.

Things to do

Best things to do in Ayutthaya

Beyond the main ruins, Ayutthaya rewards exploring by bicycle and its river setting.

  1. Cycle between the temple ruins

    The historical park's flat, spread-out layout is genuinely ideal for exploring by rented bicycle rather than walking or a hired van.

    Tip Rent a bicycle near the train station or your guesthouse — the entire historical park is genuinely manageable in a half-day by bike.

  2. Take a sunset boat tour around the island

    A longtail boat trip around the rivers encircling Ayutthaya's old city offers a genuinely different perspective on the ruins, several visible directly from the water.

    Tip Time the boat trip for sunset, when Wat Chaiwatthanaram's riverside silhouette is at its most photogenic.

  3. Visit Ayutthaya's floating market

    A smaller, more local alternative to Bangkok's famous floating markets, with vendors selling food and crafts from boats along a canal.

    Tip It's considerably less crowded than Bangkok's Damnoen Saduak, a genuinely worthwhile stop if you're already in Ayutthaya.

  4. Try Ayutthaya's boat noodles

    The city has its own genuinely strong boat noodle tradition, a rich, intensely flavoured soup traditionally sold from canal-side boats.

    Tip Order several small bowls from a boat noodle stall — they're sold in deliberately small portions for exactly this reason.

  5. Watch a traditional Thai dance performance

    Several venues near the historical park offer evening classical Thai dance shows, a genuinely worthwhile complement to the daytime ruins.

    Tip Book ahead if visiting during a peak-season weekend, when popular shows can sell out.

Local food

What to eat in Ayutthaya

Ayutthaya's food scene centres on river life, with several genuinely distinctive local specialties.

  • Boat noodles (kuay teow reua)

    Ayutthaya's own rich, dark broth noodle soup, traditionally sold from boats along the city's canals and rivers.

    Where to try it Riverside stalls and the covered boat noodle market near the old floating market area.

  • Roti sai mai

    A genuinely unique local specialty of cotton candy-like spun sugar wrapped in a thin roti, a Muslim-influenced Ayutthaya sweet found nowhere else in Thailand in quite this form.

    Where to try it Street stalls and shops near the train station, a genuine local souvenir as much as a snack.

  • River prawns (goong mae nam)

    Large freshwater prawns from Ayutthaya's rivers, grilled or in a spicy tom yum, a genuinely celebrated local specialty.

    Where to try it Riverside restaurants along the Chao Phraya and Pa Sak rivers.

  • Pad thai

    Available throughout Ayutthaya as elsewhere in Thailand, genuinely good from the city's street stalls and market vendors.

    Where to try it Street stalls around the historical park and near the train station.

  • Thai iced coffee

    Strong, sweet iced coffee with condensed milk, a genuinely welcome break while cycling between the historical park's temples in the heat.

    Where to try it Cafés and street stalls throughout the old city.

Transport

Getting to and around Ayutthaya

Ayutthaya is an easy train, bus or car trip from Bangkok, making it Thailand's most straightforward historical day trip.

Getting there

  • Train from Bangkok

    Frequent, cheap trains from Bangkok's main stations reach Ayutthaya in roughly 1.5 hours.

    Third-class tickets are genuinely inexpensive and sold on the day — no advance booking required for most trains.

  • Minivan or bus from Bangkok

    Regular minivans depart from Bangkok's Mo Chit terminal, taking about 1.5 hours.

    A genuinely faster option than the train at busy travel periods, though less scenic.

  • River boat tour from Bangkok

    Multi-hour river cruises from Bangkok combine transport with sightseeing, arriving by boat along the Chao Phraya.

    A genuinely scenic but slower option, best for those wanting the journey itself to be part of the experience.

Getting around

The historical park's flat layout makes bicycle the most practical way to see Ayutthaya's spread-out ruins.

  • Bicycle

    The most popular and practical way to reach the historical park's spread-out temple complexes.

    Rentals are widely available near the train station and most guesthouses for a genuinely modest daily fee.

  • Tuk-tuk

    A genuinely convenient option for visitors preferring not to cycle in the heat, with drivers often offering a half-day temple circuit.

    Agree the route and total fare before setting off, ideally covering the main temples in one negotiated trip.

  • Walking

    Feasible for the central cluster of temples around Wat Mahathat and Wat Phra Si Sanphet, though the wider park spreads further.

    Bring water and sun protection — shade is genuinely limited across the open ruins.

When to go

Best time to visit Ayutthaya

Ayutthaya's open, largely unshaded ruins make the cool season genuinely the most comfortable time to explore extensively by bicycle or on foot.

  • Cool season (November–February)

    Best time

    The most comfortable weather for cycling and walking between the largely unshaded temple ruins.

  • Hot season (March–May)

    Shoulder

    Genuinely intense heat across the open historical park, best managed with an early morning or late afternoon visit.

  • Rainy season (June–October)

    Good

    Typically short afternoon downpours, with thinner crowds and a genuinely atmospheric look to the ruins under grey skies.

Worth planning around: Midday hours from March to May, when the largely unshaded historical park's heat can genuinely make extensive cycling uncomfortable.

Itinerary

One to two days in Ayutthaya

The central temple ruins first, then the riverside sites and a quieter overnight stay if time allows.

  1. Day1

    The central historical park

    Ayutthaya's essential temple ruins.

    • Morning Wat Mahathat and Wat Ratchaburana, arriving early.
    • Afternoon Wat Phra Si Sanphet and the Historical Study Centre.
    • Evening A sunset boat tour around the old city island.
  2. Day2

    Riverside temples and departure

    An optional second day for the riverside sites and local specialties, if staying overnight.

    • Morning Wat Chaiwatthanaram and Wat Lokayasutharam.
    • Afternoon Try roti sai mai and river prawns before departing.
    • Evening Return to Bangkok, or onward to Chiang Mai by overnight train.

Travel tips

Ayutthaya travel tips

Practical notes on booking, timing and transport that make a real difference to an Ayutthaya trip.

  • Rent a bicycle rather than relying on a tuk-tuk alone — the historical park genuinely rewards a slower, self-paced visit.
  • Never pose for a photo taller than the Wat Mahathat Buddha head — it's disrespectful and against park rules.
  • Bring water and sun protection — shade is genuinely limited across the open ruins.
  • Visit early morning or late afternoon in the hot season to avoid the worst midday heat.
  • Combine Ayutthaya with an overnight train onward to Chiang Mai to avoid backtracking to Bangkok.
  • Buy a combined entry ticket if visiting several temples in one day — it's genuinely cheaper than paying per site.

FAQ

Ayutthaya: frequently asked questions

Is Ayutthaya worth a day trip from Bangkok?

Yes, genuinely — it's under two hours away by train and offers a UNESCO-listed historical park considerably different from Bangkok's own temples. A single day covers the main sites comfortably.

Should I stay overnight in Ayutthaya?

Worth considering if you want to see the ruins genuinely quiet in the early morning or lit at dusk without day-tripper crowds — most visitors, though, manage a satisfying visit as a single day trip from Bangkok.

How do I get around the historical park?

Bicycle is genuinely the most practical option — the park's flat, spread-out layout suits cycling better than walking, and rentals are cheap and widely available near the train station.

What is the famous Buddha head in the tree?

A sandstone Buddha head at Wat Mahathat, believed to have been left behind after the head separated from its statue and a fig tree's roots slowly grew around it over centuries — visitors must kneel, never stand taller than it, for photos.

What is the best time of year to visit?

November to February offers the most comfortable weather for cycling between the largely unshaded ruins. March to May turns genuinely hot, making an early or late visit considerably more comfortable.
