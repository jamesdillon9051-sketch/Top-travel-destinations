Why go to Thailand

Thailand earned its reputation as Southeast Asia's easiest first trip honestly: a genuinely excellent tourism infrastructure, food that ranks among the best in the world at every price point, and a range that runs from Bangkok's golden temples and chaotic markets to limestone islands rising sheer out of turquoise water, without ever requiring a visa headache or a currency most visitors can't get their head around.

That range is wider than the beach-holiday reputation suggests. The north holds the ruins of two former royal capitals — Ayutthaya and Sukhothai, both UNESCO World Heritage Sites — and a mountain culture around Chiang Mai and Chiang Rai genuinely distinct from the flat central plains. The south is where the postcard islands sit, but Kanchanaburi's WWII history and Khao Sok's ancient rainforest prove there's more inland than a transit stop between beaches.

The practical spine holding all of this together is a genuinely good network of budget flights, overnight trains and long-distance buses, plus a Buddhist culture of hospitality — sanuk, the idea that things should have an element of fun even when they're serious — that shapes how visitors are treated from a street food stall to a five-star hotel.

Experiences

Best things to do in Thailand

The experiences that recur across the country and that make a Thailand trip feel like more than a checklist of temples and beaches.

  1. Eat at a street food stall, properly

    Thailand's street food genuinely rivals its restaurants, and a plastic-stool stall with a queue of locals is one of the most reliable meals in the country.

    Tip Order what the stall specializes in rather than a broad menu — a vendor cooking one dish all day has usually mastered it.

  2. Visit a temple with real context

    Thailand's wats range from Bangkok's glittering royal temples to quiet countryside monasteries, and a little background on Thai Buddhism genuinely transforms a visit from a photo stop into something more meaningful.

    Tip Dress modestly for any temple visit — shoulders and knees covered — or you may be turned away or asked to rent a wrap at the entrance.

  3. Take an overnight train

    Thailand's overnight sleeper trains, particularly the Bangkok–Chiang Mai route, are a genuinely comfortable and atmospheric way to cover long distances while saving a night's accommodation.

    Tip Book an upper berth for more privacy, or a lower berth for the window and more room to sit up during the day.

  4. Visit a weekend or night market

    From Bangkok's sprawling Chatuchak Weekend Market to Chiang Mai's night bazaars, Thailand's markets are a genuine sensory experience of food, crafts and haggling, not just a shopping stop.

    Tip Arrive with cash in small denominations — bargaining is expected at most market stalls, but card payment usually isn't available.

  5. Take a boat past limestone karsts

    Thailand's southern coastline is defined by dramatic limestone karst formations rising straight out of the sea, best seen from a longtail boat around Phang Nga Bay, Krabi or the islands.

    Tip A sunrise or late-afternoon boat trip avoids both the midday heat and the biggest tour-boat crowds.

  6. Get a traditional Thai massage

    Genuinely central to Thai wellness culture rather than a tourist add-on, a proper Thai massage — part stretching, part pressure-point work — is available everywhere from beach-side huts to Wat Pho's famous massage school.

    Tip Wat Pho's traditional medicine school in Bangkok is where much of modern Thai massage practice is formally taught and a genuinely authentic place to try one.

Food and drink

Thailand foods to try

Thai food's international reputation undersells its own regional range — what's on offer in Bangkok, the north and the south genuinely differs more than most visitors expect.

  • Pad thai

    Stir-fried rice noodles with egg, tofu or shrimp, tamarind, fish sauce and crushed peanuts, Thailand's best-known export dish and genuinely excellent when made fresh to order.

    Where to try it Street stalls nationwide — a wok-fried version cooked to order beats a pre-made steam-tray one every time.

  • Tom yum goong

    A hot and sour prawn soup built on lemongrass, galangal, kaffir lime leaf and chilli, one of the defining flavours of Thai cuisine and genuinely more complex than its reputation as just 'spicy soup' suggests.

    Where to try it Both street stalls and proper restaurants nationwide, with genuine regional variation in how sour or creamy it's made.

  • Green curry (gaeng keow wan)

    A coconut-milk curry built on fresh green chillies, Thai basil and typically chicken or beef, genuinely one of the country's most beloved curries alongside its red and massaman cousins.

    Where to try it Restaurants and street stalls nationwide, with central Thai versions typically the reference standard.

  • Khao soi

    A northern Thai curry noodle soup topped with crispy fried noodles, genuinely distinct from central Thai cuisine and reflecting the north's Burmese and Shan culinary influence.

    Where to try it Chiang Mai and Chiang Rai specifically — it's considerably harder to find done well further south.

  • Mango sticky rice

    Sweet coconut sticky rice served with fresh mango and a drizzle of coconut cream, genuinely one of Thailand's simplest and most satisfying desserts.

    Where to try it Street dessert stalls nationwide, best in mango season from April to June.

  • Som tam (green papaya salad)

    Shredded unripe papaya pounded with chilli, lime, fish sauce and palm sugar, a genuinely fiery, sour and refreshing dish with roots in Thailand's northeastern Isan region.

    Where to try it Isan-style street stalls, which usually ask directly how spicy you want it — answer honestly.

  • Thai iced tea

    Strong black tea sweetened with condensed milk and served over ice, a genuinely essential accompaniment to a hot, spicy meal and a distinctly Thai institution.

    Where to try it Street stalls and restaurants nationwide, sold from bright orange-tinted glasses.

When to go

Best time to visit Thailand

Thailand's cool, dry season from November to February offers the most comfortable weather nationwide, though the shoulder months either side remain genuinely workable with a little more heat or rain.

  • Cool season (November–February)

    Best time

    The most comfortable weather nationwide — warm, dry days and genuinely cooler evenings, especially in the north. The peak tourist season, with prices and crowds to match.

  • Hot season (March–May)

    Good

    Genuinely intense heat, especially in central and northern Thailand, though the islands stay swimmable and crowds thin noticeably outside the Songkran holiday in April.

  • Rainy season (June–October)

    Shoulder

    Typically short, heavy afternoon downpours rather than all-day rain, with the west (Andaman) coast affected more than the Gulf coast, where Koh Samui stays comparatively dry.

  • Songkran (mid-April)

    Good

    Thailand's water festival and New Year, a genuinely joyful nationwide water fight — extraordinary fun if you're prepared to get soaked, worth avoiding if you're not.

Worth planning around: The Andaman coast (Phuket, Krabi, Khao Sok) from July to September without flexible plans — heavier rain and rougher seas than the Gulf coast can affect boat trips and snorkelling more than elsewhere.

Trip length

How long to spend in Thailand

Thailand's budget flight and overnight train network makes an ambitious north-to-south itinerary genuinely workable, though the south's islands each reward more time than a rushed loop allows.

  • 4–5 days

    Bangkok alone fills four or five days easily, or Bangkok plus a single nearby island or Ayutthaya day trip.

    A short Thailand trip is better spent deeply in one or two places than spread thin across the country.

  • 8–10 days

    Bangkok, then Chiang Mai in the north or a single island in the south — the classic first-trip split between city, culture and beach.

    Add Ayutthaya as an easy day trip from Bangkok without extending the core itinerary much.

  • 12–16 days

    The itinerary on this page: Bangkok, Ayutthaya, Chiang Mai, then flying south to Krabi and the islands, covering both the north's culture and the south's coastline.

    Enough time to add Sukhothai or Chiang Rai to a Bangkok-and-north-focused route.

  • 3 weeks or more

    Add Khao Sok, Kanchanaburi or a slower, multi-island route through Koh Samui, Koh Phangan and beyond.

    The inland nature reserves and a genuinely unhurried island-hopping pace are where a second Thailand trip belongs.

Getting around

Transportation in Thailand

Thailand's budget flight network covers long distances fast, while trains and buses cover the ground more scenically and cheaply.

  • Domestic flights

    Frequent budget routes connect Bangkok to Chiang Mai, Phuket, Krabi and Koh Samui in one to two hours.

    Book a little ahead for genuinely low fares, particularly outside peak weekends and holidays.

  • Overnight trains

    Sleeper trains connect Bangkok to Chiang Mai and Kanchanaburi, a genuinely comfortable way to cover distance while saving a night's accommodation.

    Book State Railway sleeper berths a few days ahead in peak season, when popular routes sell out.

  • Long-distance buses and minivans

    A comprehensive, affordable network reaches Ayutthaya, Sukhothai, Chiang Rai and southern destinations including Krabi reliably.

    VIP overnight buses offer reclining seats for longer routes at a genuine step up from standard service.

  • Ferries and speedboats

    Connect Phuket, Krabi and Koh Samui to their surrounding islands, with frequent daily departures in high season.

    Book through a reputable operator, as inter-island ferry schedules can be reduced in the rainy season.

  • Tuk-tuks and songthaews

    Useful for short hops within Bangkok, Chiang Mai and other cities, alongside ride-hailing apps for metered fairness.

    Agree a tuk-tuk fare before getting in, or use a ride-hailing app for a fixed, metered price instead.

Culture

Culture and etiquette in Thailand

Thai culture is shaped genuinely deeply by Buddhism and the monarchy, both treated with a level of respect visitors are expected to match, alongside a warmth and light-heartedness — sanuk — that runs through daily life.

  • The monarchy is treated with genuine, serious respect

    Thailand has strict lèse-majesté laws, and visitors should avoid any critical remarks about the royal family, in person or online.

  • The head is sacred, the feet are lowly

    Avoid touching anyone's head, even a child's, and avoid pointing your feet at people, Buddha images or temple altars.

  • Remove shoes before entering homes and temples

    A genuinely universal expectation — look for a pile of shoes at the entrance as your cue if unsure.

  • The wai greeting carries real meaning

    A slight bow with palms pressed together is Thailand's traditional greeting — visitors aren't obligated to initiate it, but returning one is polite.

  • Losing your temper causes you to lose face, not win the argument

    Public anger is genuinely seen as poor form in Thai culture — a calm, smiling approach resolves disputes considerably better than raised voices.

  • Sanuk shapes how things get done

    The idea that things should have an element of fun, even work, runs through Thai culture — a stiff, all-business approach often lands less well than a warm, easygoing one.

Do

  • Dress modestly for temples — shoulders and knees covered, shoes removed at the entrance
  • Remove your shoes before entering someone's home
  • Return a wai greeting if one is offered to you
  • Speak respectfully about the royal family and avoid the topic if in doubt
  • Bargain cheerfully at markets — it's expected and part of the interaction
  • Carry cash for street food, markets and rural areas

Don't

  • Don't touch anyone's head, even in a friendly gesture
  • Don't point your feet at people, Buddha images or temple altars
  • Don't raise your voice or lose your temper in a dispute — it backfires
  • Don't make any critical remark about the Thai monarchy, in person or online
  • Don't climb on Buddha statues or treat them as a photo prop
  • Don't assume every vendor or driver takes cards — many remain cash-only

Travel tips

Thailand travel tips

The things that most often go wrong on a Thailand trip, in rough order of how much they cost people.

  • Agree a tuk-tuk or taxi fare before getting in, or use a ride-hailing app for a fixed, metered price instead.
  • Dress modestly for temples — cover shoulders and knees, or you may be turned away or asked to rent a wrap.
  • Book domestic flights and overnight train sleepers a few days ahead in peak season, when popular routes sell out.
  • Carry cash — street food, markets, songthaews and rural areas remain genuinely cash-based.
  • Be wary of anyone approaching you about a 'closed' temple or a special gem deal — both are common tourist-area scams.
  • Wear a helmet and check your travel insurance covers scooter accidents before renting one on the islands.
  • Pack for real heat and humidity most of the year, and plan around the midday sun for sightseeing.
  • Book Phuket and Koh Samui accommodation well ahead for the cool-season peak from December to February.

FAQ

Visiting Thailand: frequently asked questions

How many days do you need in Thailand?

Ten to fourteen for a first trip. Eight to ten covers Bangkok and either the north or a single island with reasonable pacing; twelve to sixteen lets you add both Chiang Mai and the southern islands, which is the itinerary on this page. Four or five days is enough for Bangkok alone, and Bangkok alone genuinely justifies a trip.

What is the best time of year to visit Thailand?

November to February is the sweet spot — warm, dry weather nationwide and the most comfortable conditions in the north. March to May stays dry but genuinely hotter, and the rainy season from June to October brings short, heavy afternoon showers rather than all-day rain.

Do I need to fly between regions in Thailand?

Not strictly, but it helps. Budget flights connect Bangkok to Chiang Mai, Phuket and Koh Samui in one to two hours, considerably faster than the bus or train alternative, though overnight trains and buses remain a genuinely comfortable, affordable option if you have the time.

Is Thailand expensive?

Mid-range travel runs about ฿2,500–4,500 per person per day, with Phuket and Koh Samui adding a genuine premium over that figure. Eating at street stalls rather than tourist restaurants and booking domestic flights ahead are the biggest levers on cost.

Is Thailand safe for tourists?

Very. Violent crime against visitors is rare, and most areas are safe to walk in the evening. The practical risks are overcharging, scooter accidents and tourist-area scams around 'closed' temples or gem shops, manageable with ordinary skepticism of unsolicited offers.

Should I visit Bangkok, Chiang Mai or the islands first?

Bangkok, for almost everyone — it's the main international gateway, has the country's best food scene, and Ayutthaya is an easy day trip from there. Chiang Mai and the southern islands both work well as the second stop, depending on whether culture or beaches matter more to you.
