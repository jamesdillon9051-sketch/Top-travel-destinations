Welcome to London

London has been continuously settled since the Romans founded Londinium around AD 47, and the layers show, sometimes literally underfoot — a stretch of the Roman city wall still stands near the Museum of London, while the Tower of London's White Tower has watched over the city since 1078. Few capitals combine this depth of history with London's scale as a genuine present-tense world city: a financial centre, a theatre capital, and a city that absorbed more nationalities and languages into its daily life than almost anywhere else on earth.

The city's museums are, by any reasonable global standard, one of the great free cultural offerings anywhere — the British Museum, the National Gallery, the Tate Modern and the Natural History Museum all charge nothing for general admission, a legacy of a specific nineteenth-century decision that the nation's collections belonged to everyone. That alone reshapes how a London trip should be planned: the constraint is rarely money, it is time.

London is also, unavoidably, several cities in one. Westminster's palaces and government buildings sit a short walk from the City of London's Roman-founded financial square mile, itself walking distance from Shoreditch's converted warehouses and, across the river, the South Bank's cultural stretch and Borough Market's food stalls. Picking two or three neighbourhoods properly beats trying to see all of them badly.

Why visit

Why visit London?

London's case is simple: nowhere else combines this depth of continuous history with world-class free museums and a genuine present-tense global-city energy.

  • The great museums are free

    The British Museum, National Gallery, Tate Modern and Natural History Museum all charge nothing for general admission — a rare, deliberate civic decision that changes how a trip should be planned.

  • The history is genuinely continuous

    Roman wall fragments, a Norman tower, medieval Westminster Abbey and a still-functioning royal palace, all within walking distance of each other and still part of the working city.

  • The theatre is as good as it gets

    The West End rivals Broadway for quality and scale, with both blockbuster musicals and serious drama running in parallel, often at more accessible prices.

  • It is genuinely, deeply international

    Over three hundred languages are spoken in London's schools, and the food, neighbourhoods and culture reflect a city built by immigration as much as by empire.

  • Everything connects by Underground

    The Tube, whatever its reputation for delays, makes a sprawling city genuinely navigable, putting most major sights within a thirty-minute journey of each other.

Main attractions

Top things to see in London

London rewards more time than most first-time visitors budget. These eight are the ones worth building days around; the free museums alone could absorb a week.

  1. 01

    The British Museum

    Free to enter and holding several million years of human history — the Rosetta Stone, the Parthenon (Elgin) Marbles, Egyptian mummies and an Assyrian lion hunt frieze among a collection too large to see in one visit.

    Tip Focus on two or three galleries per visit rather than attempting the whole museum. The Enlightenment Gallery is a good, less-crowded starting point.

  2. 02

    The Tower of London

    A Norman fortress begun in 1078, home to the Crown Jewels, the ravens said to protect the kingdom, and nine hundred years of prisoners, executions and royal history within its walls.

    Tip Book timed tickets online in advance — it sells out on peak days and the queue for walk-up tickets can be long.

  3. 03

    Westminster Abbey and the Houses of Parliament

    A working abbey that has hosted every coronation since 1066, standing beside the Gothic Revival Houses of Parliament and Elizabeth Tower, home to the bell known as Big Ben.

    Tip Book the Abbey ahead for a specific time slot. A Parliament tour, when sitting days allow, needs booking well in advance through your MP or directly.

  4. 04

    Buckingham Palace

    The monarch's official London residence, its forecourt hosting the Changing of the Guard, with the State Rooms open to visitors only during the King's summer opening in August and September.

    Tip Check the Changing of the Guard schedule before travelling — it does not run daily, especially outside summer.

  5. 05

    The Tate Modern and the South Bank

    A former power station turned world-class modern art museum, connected by the Millennium Bridge to St Paul's Cathedral, with the South Bank's riverside walk running past the Globe Theatre and Borough Market.

    Tip Free general admission. Combine with a walk along the South Bank at golden hour for the best London skyline light.

  6. 06

    The National Gallery

    Free to enter, on Trafalgar Square, holding Van Gogh's Sunflowers, Turner, Constable and one of the world's great collections of Western European painting from the thirteenth to the twentieth century.

    Tip The collection is genuinely vast — pick up a free gallery map and plan two or three focused rooms rather than trying to see everything.

  7. 07

    The Natural History Museum and Science Museum

    Two free museums in South Kensington, side by side — a diplodocus skeleton and a blue whale under a cathedral-like Victorian roof, and hands-on science exhibits spanning space, medicine and technology.

    Tip Both are hugely popular with families — arrive at opening or accept genuine crowds by mid-morning, especially in school holidays.

  8. 08

    Shakespeare's Globe

    A faithful reconstruction of the open-air Elizabethan theatre where Shakespeare's plays were originally performed, staging his works today in the same standing-yard, thatched-roof format.

    Tip Standing tickets in the yard are cheap and give the most authentic experience — dress for the weather, as it is open to the sky.

Things to do

Best things to do in London

London rewards pairing one major sight with one neighbourhood a day, and building in real time for its free museums rather than rushing them.

  1. See a West End show

    From long-running musicals to serious new drama, London's theatre district rivals Broadway for quality, often at more accessible prices, with day-of and preview discounts available for the flexible.

    Tip Check TodayTix and official theatre box offices for day seats and returns, which can be significantly cheaper than advance full-price tickets.

  2. Eat your way through Borough Market

    A historic food market beside London Bridge, stalls selling everything from artisan cheese to street food from a dozen cuisines — genuinely one of the best lunch options in the city.

    Tip Come hungry and share dishes between stalls rather than committing to one large meal at a single vendor.

  3. Walk a royal park

    Hyde Park, Regent's Park, St James's Park and Greenwich Park all offer genuine green space and quiet within the city, a useful counterweight to London's density and pace.

    Tip Greenwich adds the Royal Observatory and the Prime Meridian line to the park itself — worth the DLR trip out.

  4. Ride the Underground like a local

    The Tube is the fastest way to cover London's real distances, and a contactless card or Oyster card works seamlessly across it, buses and the Overground.

    Tip Avoid rush hour (roughly 8–9:30am and 5–6:30pm) if you can — the network is genuinely crowded at peak times.

  5. See the city from the river

    A Thames river boat between Westminster and Greenwich or the Tower gives a different, unhurried perspective on the skyline and passes most of the city's major landmarks in sequence.

    Tip Travelcards and Oyster pay-as-you-go discounts often apply to certain river bus services, not just the Tube and buses.

  6. Explore beyond Zone 1

    Neighbourhoods like Shoreditch, Notting Hill, Camden and Greenwich each have their own distinct character, markets and food scenes well worth the short Tube ride from central London.

    Tip Camden Market and Portobello Road (Notting Hill) are both best visited on their specific market days for the full atmosphere.

Local food

What to eat in London

London's food scene reflects its population as much as its own traditions — genuinely excellent British, and genuinely excellent everything else besides.

  • Sunday roast at a proper pub

    Roast meat, potatoes, vegetables and Yorkshire pudding, the definitive London Sunday institution, best had at a proper pub rather than a chain restaurant.

    Where to try it Neighbourhood pubs across the city — ask locally, since the best ones are rarely the most central or famous.

  • Fish and chips

    Battered fish and chips, done properly, remains one of London's best simple meals, though quality varies enormously between a good chippy and a tourist-trap one.

    Where to try it Poppies in Spitalfields or a genuinely local chippy away from the main tourist strips.

  • A full English breakfast

    Eggs, bacon, sausage, beans, grilled tomato and toast, a genuine daily ritual across the city's cafes rather than a tourist performance.

    Where to try it Traditional 'greasy spoon' cafes across the city, particularly in the East End.

  • Curry on Brick Lane

    London's Bangladeshi and wider South Asian food scene, concentrated historically around Brick Lane, represents one of the city's most significant culinary contributions and remains genuinely excellent.

    Where to try it Brick Lane's curry houses, though increasingly it is worth exploring beyond the main strip for the best value and quality.

  • Afternoon tea

    Finger sandwiches, scones with jam and clotted cream, and pastries, served formally over several courses — a genuine London institution rather than just a tourist photo opportunity.

    Where to try it Traditional hotels for the full formal experience, or more affordable versions at independent tearooms across the city.

  • Street food markets

    Beyond Borough Market, markets across the city showcase London's food diversity — everything from Ethiopian to Filipino to modern British, changing with the city's population.

    Where to try it Borough Market, Maltby Street Market and Broadway Market, each with a slightly different character and vendor mix.

Accommodation

Where to stay in London

London is large enough that neighbourhood genuinely shapes the trip. The choice is mainly between central convenience and a more local, better-value stay a short Tube ride out.

  • Covent Garden / West End

    Central, walking distance to the major sights and theatres, and on multiple Tube lines. Expensive, but you step out into the action rather than commuting to it.

    Best for: First visits and theatre

  • South Bank / Southwark

    Walking distance to the Tate Modern, Borough Market and Shakespeare's Globe, with the City and Westminster a short walk across the river.

    Best for: Museums and river views

  • Shoreditch / East London

    A livelier, younger district with independent restaurants and bars, a Tube or Overground ride from central sights but often better hotel value.

    Best for: Nightlife and value

  • Kensington / South Kensington

    Walking distance to the Natural History Museum, Science Museum and V&A, a genteel residential area well connected by Tube.

    Best for: Museums and quiet

Transport

Getting to and around London

London has multiple major airports and is the natural hub of Britain's rail network, making it the default start or end of most UK trips.

Getting there

  • Heathrow Airport (LHR)

    The main international gateway, connected to central London by the fast Elizabeth line or Heathrow Express, both reaching the centre in around 15–45 minutes depending on the line and destination station.

    The Elizabeth line is usually the better-value option; Heathrow Express is faster to Paddington specifically but pricier.

  • Gatwick, Stansted and Luton Airports

    Serve mainly budget and some long-haul carriers, each connected to central London by a dedicated express train taking 30–55 minutes.

    Check which airport your flight actually uses — they are well outside the city and not interchangeable for onward plans.

  • Eurostar

    High-speed rail from Paris, Brussels and Amsterdam arriving directly into St Pancras International in central London.

    Book ahead for the cheapest fares, and allow time for the airport-style security and border checks before boarding.

  • Inter-city rail

    London's main stations connect to the whole country: Bath 1h30, York 2h, Edinburgh 4h30.

    Book via Trainline or National Rail. Different destinations depart from different London stations — check which one before travelling.

Getting around

London's Underground, buses and Overground form one of the world's most extensive urban transport networks, covering nearly every sight in this guide.

  • The Underground (Tube)

    Eleven lines covering the city, fast and frequent though it can be crowded and occasionally delayed. The single best way to cover London's real distances.

    Use a contactless card or Oyster card for pay-as-you-go, automatically capped at the best daily fare for your zones travelled.

  • Buses

    A dense network reaching areas the Tube does not, and a genuinely pleasant way to see street-level London, particularly from the top deck.

    Buses are cashless — tap the same contactless or Oyster card used on the Tube.

  • Walking within central zones

    Zone 1's core sights are closer together than the Tube map suggests — Covent Garden to Trafalgar Square, for instance, is a ten-minute walk that looks like a Tube journey on the map.

    A well-connected walking app or paper map helps avoid unnecessary short Tube hops.

  • Black cabs and ride-hailing apps

    Licensed black cabs are iconic but expensive; Uber and similar apps offer a cheaper, if less characterful, alternative for point-to-point journeys.

    Black cab drivers famously pass 'The Knowledge', an exam on London's streets — worth chatting to one at least once.

When to go

Best time to visit London

London's museums and theatres work year-round, which makes weather and crowds, rather than opening seasons, the main factors in timing a visit.

  • Spring (April–May)

    Best time

    Mild, with parks and gardens at their best and crowds still below the summer peak. April can be genuinely wet, so pack accordingly.

  • Summer (June–August)

    Good

    The best odds of warm weather and long daylight hours, alongside the year's biggest crowds and highest hotel prices, particularly in August.

  • Autumn (September–October)

    Best time

    Often the best-kept secret: comfortable temperatures, autumn colour in the parks, and noticeably thinner crowds than summer.

  • Winter (November–February)

    Shoulder

    Short, often grey days, but the Christmas lights, markets and West End pantomime season give the city a genuine seasonal atmosphere, and hotel prices drop outside the December peak.

Worth planning around: Late July and August's school-holiday crush pushes prices and crowds to their annual peak at the major sights and museums.

Itinerary

Four days in London

One major sight and one neighbourhood a day, with real time built in for the free museums rather than a rushed pass-through.

  1. Day1

    Westminster and the river

    The seat of government, then the South Bank.

    • Morning Westminster Abbey and the Houses of Parliament from outside, or booked inside.
    • Afternoon Walk the South Bank to the Tate Modern.
    • Evening Dinner near Borough Market.
  2. Day2

    The great museums

    A full day given to the British Museum and Trafalgar Square.

    • Morning The British Museum, focused on two or three galleries.
    • Afternoon The National Gallery on Trafalgar Square.
    • Evening A West End show, booked ahead.
  3. Day3

    The Tower and the City

    Royal history, then London's financial heart.

    • Morning The Tower of London, booked ahead, including the Crown Jewels.
    • Afternoon A walk through the City to St Paul's Cathedral.
    • Evening Dinner in Shoreditch.
  4. Day4

    Royal London and South Kensington

    The palace, then the museum quarter.

    • Morning Buckingham Palace and the Changing of the Guard, if scheduled.
    • Afternoon The Natural History Museum or Science Museum.
    • Evening A last dinner and, if time allows, one more neighbourhood walk.

Travel tips

London travel tips

London-specific practicalities, most of which are about booking, transport cards and the city's genuine scale.

  • Book the Tower of London, popular West End shows and any Parliament tour well in advance — all three sell out or spike in price close to the date.
  • Use a contactless bank card or an Oyster card for all public transport — it automatically caps your daily fare and is cheaper than single paper tickets.
  • London's free museums genuinely deserve real time — treat the British Museum, National Gallery and Natural History Museum as full mornings or afternoons, not quick stops.
  • Check which airport your flight uses when planning transport — Heathrow, Gatwick, Stansted, Luton and City are all genuinely far apart and not interchangeable.
  • The Tube gets genuinely crowded during weekday rush hour — avoid roughly 8–9:30am and 5–6:30pm with luggage if you can.
  • Rain is a real possibility in any month — carry a compact umbrella or waterproof layer regardless of the season.
  • Central London's core sights are closer together on foot than the Tube map suggests — check walking distances before automatically taking the Underground for short hops.
  • August and Christmas week are the most expensive and crowded times to visit — book accommodation well ahead if travelling then.

FAQ

London: frequently asked questions

How many days do you need in London?

Four to six. That gives a day for Westminster and the South Bank, a full day for the major free museums, a day for the Tower of London and the City, and a day for Buckingham Palace and South Kensington's museums. Three days is possible but rushed given how much is genuinely free and worth lingering in.

What do I need to book in advance in London?

The Tower of London, which sells out on peak days. Popular West End shows, especially long-running hits. Any Parliament tour, which needs booking well ahead. The great museums — British Museum, National Gallery, Natural History Museum — are free and generally do not need advance booking, though special exhibitions within them often do.

When is the best time to visit London?

May to June and September. Both offer mild weather and manageable crowds. Summer brings the best odds of sunshine and the biggest crowds; winter is atmospheric around Christmas but cold, grey and short on daylight.

Where should I stay in London?

Covent Garden or the West End for first visits and theatre access. South Bank or Southwark for museums and river views. Shoreditch for nightlife and better value. Kensington for the museum quarter and a quieter residential feel. All are well connected by Tube.

Is London expensive?

Yes, genuinely the most expensive city in this guide, running roughly £150–280 per person per day mid-range. The free museums offset this significantly — a huge part of what makes London worth visiting costs nothing to see.

Is London safe?

Yes, with ordinary vigilance. Violent crime against visitors is rare. Pickpocketing occurs in crowded tourist areas and on the Underground — keep bags zipped and phones out of back pockets, and the practical risk largely disappears.

What should I eat in London?

A Sunday roast at a proper pub, fish and chips away from the tourist strips, and curry on or near Brick Lane, one of the city's most significant culinary contributions. Afternoon tea for a genuine occasion, and the food markets — Borough above all — for a cross-section of everything the city now cooks.
