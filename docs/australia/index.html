Why go to Australia

Australia is the sixth-largest country in the world, roughly the size of the contiguous United States, but with a population of only about 27 million, most of them living in a handful of cities on the coast. Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples have lived here for at least 60,000 years, making theirs the oldest continuous living cultures on earth, and their art, stories and connection to Country are increasingly part of the visitor experience. European settlement began in 1788 at Sydney, and the country became a federation in 1901.

For visitors, the country has some of the most varied natural attractions anywhere: the Great Barrier Reef, the tropical rainforest of the Daintree, the red centre around Uluru, the wildernesses of Tasmania and Kakadu, and thousands of kilometres of beaches. The cities are equally appealing, with Sydney's harbour, Melbourne's cafés and laneways and Perth's beaches and sunshine. Australians are relaxed, friendly and outdoorsy, and the infrastructure is excellent.

The main challenge is distance. Sydney to Perth is about as far as London to Baghdad, and Sydney to Cairns is a three-hour flight. It is best to choose a few regions and fly between them, rather than trying to see everything. Prices are also high by international standards, and seasons are reversed from the northern hemisphere, so summer runs from December to February.

Experiences

Best things to do in Australia

The experiences that recur across the country and make an Australian trip feel like one.

  1. Snorkel or dive the Great Barrier Reef

    The world's largest coral reef system is a marvel, with colourful fish, turtles and coral gardens, reached by boat from Cairns, Port Douglas or the Whitsundays. See Cairns and the Reef.

    Tip Choose an eco-certified operator, and a trip to the outer reef, where the coral is healthiest.

  2. See a kangaroo or koala in the wild

    Kangaroos, wallabies and koalas are common in national parks, on golf courses and along the coast, especially at dawn and dusk. Wildlife sanctuaries offer close encounters.

    Tip Drive slowly at dusk, when animals cross roads.

  3. Learn from Aboriginal culture

    Guided walks with Aboriginal guides at Uluru, Kakadu and elsewhere share stories, bush foods and rock art that are tens of thousands of years old. See Uluru and Kakadu.

    Tip Choose Aboriginal-owned tours, and follow local rules about sacred sites and photography.

  4. Take a coastal road trip

    Australia is made for road trips, from the Great Ocean Road to the Pacific Highway and the west coast. See the Great Ocean Road.

    Tip Drive on the left, take breaks every two hours and never drive tired.

  5. Swim, surf and lounge on the beach

    There are more than 10,000 beaches, from Bondi's surf to Whitehaven's white sand. See Sydney and the Whitsundays.

    Tip Swim between the red and yellow flags, patrolled by lifesavers.

  6. Ride a great train journey

    The Ghan (Adelaide to Darwin) and the Indian Pacific (Sydney to Perth) are among the world's great rail journeys, crossing the continent in comfort.

    Tip Book months ahead, and allow several days.

  7. Wander a market and try a flat white

    Melbourne and Sydney's cafés, laneways and food markets are among the best in the world, and Australian coffee culture has spread across the globe. See Melbourne.

    Tip Order a flat white or a long black.

Food and drink

Australia foods to try

Modern Australian cooking is a mix of British roots, Mediterranean and Asian influence and excellent local produce, from seafood and beef to wine and coffee. Each region has its specialities.

  • Meat pie

    A flaky pastry filled with minced beef and gravy, and the classic Aussie snack, often with tomato sauce. Bakeries in small towns compete for the best.

    Where to try it Bakeries and footy stadiums, especially in Melbourne.

  • Flat white

    Espresso with velvety steamed milk, invented in Australia or New Zealand, depending on who you ask. Melbourne and Sydney cafés serve some of the best coffee anywhere.

    Where to try it Cafés in Melbourne's laneways and Sydney's suburbs.

  • Lamington

    Squares of sponge cake dipped in chocolate and rolled in desiccated coconut, a national sweet often filled with cream and jam.

    Where to try it Bakeries and cafés across the country.

  • Pavlova

    A crisp meringue with a soft centre, topped with cream and fresh fruit, a summer and Christmas dessert.

    Where to try it Cafés and family tables, especially at Christmas.

  • Barramundi and fresh seafood

    Barramundi from the tropical north, Moreton Bay bugs, Tasmanian oysters and Western Australian rock lobster are among the many local specialities.

    Where to try it Waterfront restaurants in the Top End, Tasmania and Sydney.

  • Wine

    Australia produces excellent wine, from Barossa shiraz to Margaret River cabernet and Yarra Valley pinot noir. Many regions offer tastings within easy reach of cities.

    Where to try it Wineries in Margaret River and near Melbourne.

Before you go

Practical travel information for Australia

The essentials, current for 2026. Australia is very easy to visit, though distances, sun and prices need planning.

Capital
Canberra
Language
English. Australian English is informal, with plenty of slang, and there are many Aboriginal languages spoken in communities.
Currency
Australian dollar (AUD, A$). Roughly A$1.50 to the US dollar in recent years.
Cards and cash
Cards and contactless payments are accepted almost everywhere, including small shops and taxis. ATMs are common in cities but scarce in remote areas, so carry some cash outback. Prices in shops are quoted without tips, and tax is included.
Entry and visas
Most visitors need a visa or electronic authority before travel, usually an ETA (subclass 601), applied for through the official Australian app or an eVisitor for some European passports. New Zealand citizens do not need one. Check the official Australian government site for your passport.
Power sockets
Type I, 230 V, 50 Hz.
Emergency number
000 for police, fire and ambulance (112 also works from mobiles).
Tipping
Not expected, since wages are high, though rounding up or leaving 10% for great service is appreciated in restaurants.
Safety
Australia is very safe, but nature is powerful. Wear sunscreen and a hat, since the UV is intense; swim between the flags, since rips are common; do not swim in northern waters where saltwater crocodiles live or in the stinger season without protection; and carry water in the outback. In bushfire season, check warnings and follow local advice.
Internet and SIM
Excellent in cities and along the main highways. Coverage is patchy or absent in remote areas. Local SIM and eSIM plans are cheap.
Time zone
Three main zones: Eastern (UTC+10), Central (UTC+9:30) and Western (UTC+8), with daylight saving in some states from October to April.

When to go

Best time to visit Australia

Australia's climate ranges from tropical in the north to temperate in the south, and the seasons are reversed from the northern hemisphere. The north has a dry season from May to October and a wet season from November to April.

  • Summer (December–February)

    Good

    Hot in the south and warm on the beaches, but wet, humid and stinger-prone in the tropical north, and very hot in the centre. Peak domestic holidays.

  • Autumn (March–May)

    Best time

    Warm and settled in the south, with fewer crowds. The north dries out from April.

  • Winter (June–August)

    Best time

    Mild and sunny in the north and centre, the best time for the reef, Uluru and Kakadu. Cool in the south, with snow in the mountains.

  • Spring (September–November)

    Best time

    Warm, sunny and green in the south, with wildflowers in Western Australia.

Worth planning around: The tropical north from January to March (cyclones, floods and stingers), and the Red Centre in summer, when temperatures often exceed 40°C.

Trip length

How long to spend in Australia

Australia is enormous, and flights eat time. Two weeks allow for three or four bases, and three weeks add a slower pace or a road trip.

  • 7–8 days

    Sydney with the Blue Mountains, and a few days on the Great Barrier Reef.

    A first taste with one flight.

  • 12–14 days

    The itinerary on this page: Sydney, the Blue Mountains, Cairns, Uluru, Melbourne and the Great Ocean Road.

    The classic first trip, with four flights.

  • 3 weeks

    Adds Tasmania, the Whitsundays or Perth.

    A more relaxed pace.

  • 4 weeks or more

    Adds Kakadu, the west coast and a road trip.

    For those with time to explore.

Getting around

Transportation in Australia

Distances mean that most visitors combine domestic flights with hire cars, and use public transport within the cities.

  • Domestic flights

    Qantas, Virgin Australia, Jetstar and Rex link all major cities and tourist regions. Flights are frequent, and often cheap if booked ahead.

    Book early for school holidays. Budget carriers charge for bags.

  • Hire cars and campervans

    The best way to see coastlines and national parks. Drive on the left. Distances between towns can be huge, and fuel stops far apart in the outback.

    Book insurance carefully, and avoid driving at dusk and dawn when kangaroos are active.

  • Trains

    City trains are good in Sydney, Melbourne and Perth, and long-distance services include the Ghan, the Indian Pacific and the Overland. Journeys are slow but scenic.

    Book scenic trains months ahead.

  • Coaches and tours

    Coach services cover the coasts, and organised tours are the easiest way to reach Uluru, Kakadu and the Reef.

    Small-group tours suit solo travellers.

  • City transport

    Sydney, Melbourne and Perth have trams, trains, buses and ferries on integrated smartcards. Ride-hailing is widespread.

    Use contactless payment or the local card.

Culture

Culture and etiquette in Australia

Australia is a multicultural, egalitarian and informal country. People are direct and friendly, use first names and expect good humour. Respect for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, and awareness of the land's history, is an important part of modern Australian life.

  • Be relaxed and friendly

    Australians value informality and a sense of humour. Chat with people and do not stand on ceremony.

  • Respect Aboriginal culture

    Follow local rules about sacred sites and ask before photographing people. Choose Aboriginal-led tours where possible.

  • Queue and wait your turn

    Queuing is taken seriously, in shops, bars and at bus stops.

  • Shout your round

    In pubs, people take turns buying rounds of drinks for the group. Join in.

  • Stay safe in the sun and sea

    Follow the lifesavers' advice, wear sunscreen, and take signs about wildlife seriously.

  • Look after the environment

    Take your rubbish with you, do not feed wildlife and stay on marked tracks.

Do

  • Wear a hat and sunscreen, all year
  • Swim between the flags
  • Book accommodation ahead in school holidays
  • Learn a few local terms, such as 'arvo' (afternoon) and 'no worries'
  • Respect signs about crocodiles and closed tracks
  • Carry plenty of water in remote areas

Don't

  • Don't climb Uluru, which has been closed to climbing since 2019
  • Don't swim in northern rivers or estuaries without a safe-swimming sign
  • Don't drive tired or at dusk in rural areas
  • Don't feed wild animals
  • Don't bring in fresh food, plants or animal products without declaring them
  • Don't photograph or enter sacred sites where signs ask you not to

Travel tips

Australia travel tips

The things that most often catch visitors out in Australia, several of which are specific to how the country works.

  • Be realistic about distances. Choose three or four bases and fly between them.
  • Wear sunscreen, a hat and sunglasses every day, even when it is cloudy.
  • Swim only at patrolled beaches, between the flags, and check for rips.
  • Book Uluru, the Reef and popular tours well ahead in high season.
  • Fill up with fuel and water before long drives in remote areas.
  • Drive on the left, and be careful of animals at dawn and dusk.
  • Declare all food and outdoor gear at the border. Biosecurity rules are strict, and fines are large.
  • Check bushfire, flood and cyclone warnings, particularly from November to April.

FAQ

Visiting Australia: frequently asked questions

Is Australia safe?

Yes, it is very safe for visitors. The biggest risks are the sun, the surf, driving and the outdoors, which is best handled with common sense and local advice.

How many days do you need in Australia?

Two weeks for a first trip, and three if you can. A week lets you do Sydney and one other place.

What is the best time to visit Australia?

It depends where. Spring and autumn suit the south, and May to October suits the north and the Red Centre.

Do I need a visa for Australia?

Most visitors need an ETA or eVisitor, applied for online before travel. Check the official government site.

Is Australia expensive?

Yes, by many standards, particularly for accommodation, food and internal flights. Camping, self-catering and off-peak travel help.

Are the animals dangerous?

Some are, but attacks are rare. Take care with crocodiles in the north, marine stingers in summer and snakes and spiders in the bush, and follow local advice.

Can I climb Uluru?

No. The climb was closed permanently in October 2019, at the request of the Anangu traditional owners. You can walk around the base.
