The West Bank holds Bethlehem, Jericho and Hebron, among the oldest towns in the world, alongside terraced valleys, desert monasteries and cities with lively food scenes, together with a complex political reality. This guide covers how the territory works on the ground, how to choose between the ten destinations, how to move around, what to expect at checkpoints and how to travel responsibly.

How the West Bank works on the ground

The West Bank covers about 5,600 square kilometres, roughly 130 km from north to south and up to 60 km from east to west. It lies between the Jordan River and the 1949 armistice line, and has been under Israeli military control since 1967. It is home to about three million Palestinians and, in Israeli settlements, about half a million Israelis, which are considered illegal under international law by most of the world, though Israel disputes this.

Under the Oslo Accords, the territory was divided into Areas A, B and C. Palestinian cities, including Bethlehem, Ramallah, Nablus, Jericho and much of Hebron, are in Area A. The countryside, the Jordan Valley and the land around the settlements are largely in Area C. In practice, this means Israeli checkpoints and roads, restrictions on movement for Palestinians, and separate systems for settlers and residents.

For visitors, the result is a landscape of contrasts: welcoming towns and villages, roads with different rules for different drivers, checkpoints of varying strictness, and a separation barrier that runs along and through parts of the territory. Visitors with foreign passports have much more freedom than Palestinians, but conditions still vary from day to day.

It helps to travel with an open mind, accept that journeys are uncertain, and listen to people's own accounts of what they experience.

Worth knowing: Nothing in this guide is a substitute for your government's current advice, which is more up to date than any guidebook.

Choosing between the ten destinations

  • If you have one day

    Bethlehem, as a day trip from Jerusalem: the Church of the Nativity, the old town and lunch. Add Herodium or Battir if you have a driver.

  • If you have three days

    Bethlehem, Jericho and the Mar Saba monastery, with a walk at Battir. These are the most accessible and the most visited.

  • If you are interested in food and culture

    Ramallah for restaurants, galleries and the music scene, Nablus for knafeh and soap, and Taybeh for beer and village life.

  • If you are interested in archaeology

    Jericho for ten thousand years of habitation, Herodium for Herod's mountain palace, and Sebastia for the Roman and Israelite city of Samaria.

  • If you want to understand the politics

    Hebron, with a local guide, and Ramallah for museums and conversations. Both need careful checks on current conditions.

  • If you like walking

    Battir for the terraces, the Wadi Qelt near Jericho, and the Masar Ibrahim al-Khalil trail that crosses the West Bank.

Getting in and moving around

All entry to the West Bank is through Israeli-controlled crossings. Most visitors arrive from Jerusalem, through the Checkpoint 300 to Bethlehem or the Qalandiya checkpoint to Ramallah, and some come through the Allenby/King Hussein Bridge from Jordan. There is no airport, and there are no direct international connections.

Service taxis and buses. Palestinian shared vans and minibuses run between towns and are cheap. They do not run on a strict timetable, and drivers wait until they are full.

Private drivers. The most comfortable and flexible option, and often no more expensive than a hire car. A driver knows the checkpoints and can act as a guide.

Hire cars. Israeli rental companies generally forbid driving in Area A and their insurance will not cover you. Palestinian agencies offer rentals, but you need to check the rules and understand the road system.

Checkpoints. They range from a wave-through to hours of delay. Carry your passport, be polite, and allow extra time. Do not photograph the soldiers or the checkpoints.

Worth knowing: Do not drive at night in unfamiliar areas, and check the road conditions with your host or driver before setting out.

When to go

The hills of the West Bank have a Mediterranean climate, with a warm dry summer and a cool wet winter. Jericho and the Jordan Valley are in the desert and are far hotter.

March to May and September to November are the best months. The hills are green in spring, and the olive harvest in October and November is a memorable time to visit, when families pick and press olives together.

Summer is hot but manageable in the hills, and very hot in Jericho. Winter is cool and wet, and sometimes snowy, and Jericho is at its best. Christmas in Bethlehem draws pilgrims and celebrations, and is also the busiest and most expensive time to visit.

Check the calendar. Ramadan changes opening hours and the atmosphere, and the Eid holidays close offices and change transport. Israeli holidays can also close checkpoints and crossings.

Worth knowing: Political events and security operations can change access at any time, and the weather is only one factor in when to go.

Travelling responsibly

Tourism is a significant part of the Palestinian economy, particularly in Bethlehem, and visitors are widely welcomed. Where you stay, eat and shop matters.

Stay in Palestinian-owned guesthouses and hotels, eat in local restaurants, and hire Palestinian guides and drivers. Buy crafts, olive oil and soap from cooperatives and workshops.

Listen to a range of perspectives. Palestinian, Israeli and international guides and organisations offer different accounts, and hearing more than one gives you a fuller picture.

Respect local customs, dress modestly and ask before photographing people. Be honest with border officials, and do not carry anything you would not want examined.

Three routes that work

  1. One day: Bethlehem from Jerusalem

    Cross at Checkpoint 300, visit the Church of the Nativity and the Milk Grotto, have lunch in the old town and return by mid-afternoon. It is the standard day trip and gives a taste of the territory.

    Tip Carry your passport, and check conditions at the checkpoint before you go.

  2. Three days: Bethlehem, the desert and Jericho

    Bethlehem on day one, Battir, Herodium and Mar Saba on day two, and Jericho on day three. It combines the main religious sites, the hills and the desert.

    Tip Stay in Bethlehem for two nights, and travel with a driver.

  3. One week: the whole territory

    The itinerary on the country page, from Bethlehem and Jericho through Ramallah, Taybeh, Nablus and Sebastia to Hebron. It shows the range of the territory, if conditions allow.

    Tip Be ready to skip parts of the itinerary if there are closures or if advice changes.

FAQ

Planning a trip to the Palestinian Territories

Can I visit Bethlehem on a day trip from Jerusalem?

Yes, and most visitors do. Bus 231 and taxis run from near Damascus Gate to Bethlehem's Checkpoint 300, where foreign visitors are usually waved through. Staying a night gives you a much better sense of the town, and access can change at short notice.

What are Areas A, B and C?

Divisions created by the Oslo Accords in the 1990s. In Area A, the Palestinian Authority has civil and security control, and this covers the main cities. In Area B, the Palestinian Authority has civil control and Israel has security control. In Area C, about 60% of the West Bank, Israel has full control, and this includes most of the countryside and several archaeological sites.

Is it better to hire a local guide?

Usually, yes. A local guide knows the roads and checkpoints, can arrange access, and can explain the history and politics on the ground. For Hebron in particular, a guide is strongly recommended, and there are guides from a range of perspectives.

Should I visit if I am worried about safety?

It is a personal decision, and you should read your government's advice first. Many visitors travel without incident and describe the hospitality as extraordinary, but conditions have worsened since 2023 and advice is stricter for some areas than others.
