Welcome to Hebron

Hebron, called al-Khalil in Arabic, is the largest city in the West Bank, with about 200,000 people, and one of the oldest continuously inhabited cities in the world. It sits in the Judean hills, 35 km south of Jerusalem, and it is a commercial centre known for its glass, pottery, leather, marble and grapes. Its name in Arabic, 'the Friend', refers to Abraham, whom the Quran calls the friend of God.

At its heart is the Ibrahimi Mosque, known to Jews as the Cave of the Patriarchs, a large stone enclosure built by Herod the Great over a cave traditionally regarded as the burial place of Abraham, Isaac, Jacob and their wives. It is holy to Muslims, Jews and Christians, and it has been the focus of tension for a long time. UNESCO inscribed the Old City of Hebron on the World Heritage List in 2017, and on the list of sites in danger, a decision that Israel and the United States opposed.

Hebron is also unique in the West Bank. Under the 1997 Hebron Protocol, the city was divided into an area administered by the Palestinian Authority (H1) and an area under Israeli control (H2), which includes the Old City and the Ibrahimi Mosque. A small number of Israeli settlers, several hundred, live in H2 amid tens of thousands of Palestinians, protected by a large military presence. Palestinians and human rights groups describe severe restrictions on movement and daily life, including closed streets and shops; Israeli officials and settlers cite security threats and historical Jewish ties to the city. Visitors see this directly, and it is worth going with a local guide who can explain what you see.

Why visit

Why visit Hebron?

Hebron is not an easy or comfortable place to visit, and it is one of the most revealing.

  • A shrine holy to three faiths

    The Ibrahimi Mosque is a Herodian-era enclosure with Mamluk and Ottoman additions, and it is a functioning mosque and synagogue, divided between the two communities.

  • A Mamluk and Ottoman old city

    Stone alleys, markets and arches from the 13th to 19th centuries make up one of the best-preserved old cities in the West Bank.

  • Traditional crafts

    Hebron's blue glass, ceramics and leather goods are famous across Palestine, and some workshops welcome visitors.

  • Understanding the conflict on the ground

    Nowhere else shows so plainly how the occupation affects daily life, and hearing from residents gives a perspective that reports cannot.

  • Warm hospitality

    Even in difficult conditions, people are generous and welcoming to visitors.

Main attractions

Top things to see in Hebron

The main sights are in the Old City, and access varies with checkpoints and closures. Visit with a local guide.

  1. 01

    The Ibrahimi Mosque (Cave of the Patriarchs)

    A massive stone enclosure built by Herod, with a mosque inside, where the cenotaphs of Abraham, Isaac, Jacob and their wives can be seen. Since a massacre of 29 Muslim worshippers by a Jewish settler in 1994, it has been divided into a mosque and a synagogue, with separate entrances.

    Tip Access is through Israeli security checks and can be restricted on Fridays and on Jewish and Muslim holidays. Dress modestly, and check the hours.

  2. 02

    The Old City souk

    A network of narrow covered lanes with fruit, spice and clothing stalls, some with nets overhead that catch rubbish thrown from above. Many shops in the streets closest to the mosque have been closed since the 1990s and 2000s.

    Tip Go with a local guide, and buy from the shops that are open.

  3. 03

    Shuhada Street

    Once the main commercial street of Hebron, it has been largely closed to Palestinians since the 1990s, with a heavy military presence. It is often the focus of guided tours.

    Tip A guide is essential. Do not photograph soldiers or checkpoints.

  4. 04

    Glass and pottery workshops

    Family workshops produce blue Hebron glass and painted ceramics, using techniques that go back generations. Visitors can watch the glassblowers and buy pieces.

    Tip Buying directly supports the families.

  5. 05

    The Tel Rumeida area

    A hillside neighbourhood in H2 where an archaeological site, a small Israeli settlement and Palestinian homes sit close together. It shows the city's divisions clearly.

    Tip Visits are usually part of a guided tour.

  6. 06

    The Qaitbay Mosque and old city buildings

    Mamluk-era mosques, madrasas and merchants' houses survive in parts of the Old City, and some have been restored with international support.

    Tip Ask your guide which are open.

  7. 07

    The Abraham's Oak (Tree of Abraham) area

    A site just outside the city, revered in tradition as the place where Abraham received three angels, with a Russian Orthodox monastery and an ancient tree.

    Tip Check access and opening hours.

Things to do

Best things to do in Hebron

Visit with care and with a local guide, and be ready for the possibility that access will be limited.

  1. Take a guided walk of the Old City

    Palestinian, Israeli and international organisations run tours that give different accounts of what you see. Hearing more than one is worthwhile.

    Tip Book in advance, and check which organisation runs the tour and its perspective.

  2. Visit a glass or pottery workshop

    See craftspeople at work and buy a piece to take home. It is a good way to support local families.

    Tip Ask about workshops in H1, outside the Old City.

  3. Eat a Hebron meal

    Local restaurants serve dishes such as qidreh (rice and lamb baked in a clay pot), musakhan and freshly grilled meats.

    Tip Ask your guide for a family-run place.

  4. Taste Hebron grapes and sweets

    The region is known for its grapes, and shops sell grape molasses (dibs) and dried fruit.

    Tip Try the sweets in the central market.

  5. Talk to residents

    Many local people, from shopkeepers to guides, are happy to speak about life in the city and their perspective.

    Tip Listen respectfully, and do not argue.

  6. Visit the Abraham's Oak area

    A peaceful place outside the city with a monastery garden.

    Tip Check access before you go.

Local food

What to eat in Hebron

Hebron is known for its rich, meat-based cooking, and its grapes and sweets are famous across Palestine.

  • Qidreh

    A Hebron speciality: lamb and rice with chickpeas and spices, slow-cooked in a sealed clay pot in a wood-fired oven. Traditionally served at gatherings.

    Where to try it Family restaurants in H1, ideally arranged by your guide.

  • Musakhan

    Roast chicken with sumac and onions on taboon bread.

    Where to try it Restaurants and family homes.

  • Hebron grapes and dibs

    Hebron's grapes are famous, and the grape syrup, dibs, is used in sweets and drinks.

    Where to try it Markets and shops.

  • Falafel and hummus

    The reliable staple, good and cheap.

    Where to try it Stalls near the central market.

  • Knafeh

    Hot cheese pastry with syrup, sold by weight from big trays.

    Where to try it Sweet shops in the centre.

  • Arabic coffee and tea

    Strong, cardamom-scented coffee and sweet tea are offered wherever you go.

    Where to try it Shops and workshops.

Accommodation

Where to stay in Hebron

Few visitors stay in Hebron, and most come as a day trip from Jerusalem or Bethlehem. A small number of guesthouses and hotels operate in H1.

  • H1 (Palestinian-administered area)

    A few simple hotels and guesthouses in the modern centre, away from the Old City.

    Best for: A night in the city

  • Bethlehem or Jerusalem

    A wider range of accommodation, and a much shorter trip to Hebron than from Tel Aviv.

    Best for: Most visitors

Transport

Getting to and around Hebron

Hebron is about 35 km south of Jerusalem and 30 km south of Bethlehem. Access can change at short notice.

Getting there

  • Service taxi from Bethlehem or Jerusalem

    Shared vans run from Bethlehem's bus station and from Jerusalem, taking about 45 minutes to an hour, depending on checkpoints.

    Ask about the latest route and timetable.

  • Private driver or tour

    A Palestinian driver or a guided tour is the easiest and often the safest way to visit.

    Agree the itinerary in advance.

  • Checkpoints

    Israeli checkpoints control access to the Old City and to H2. Foreign passport holders are usually admitted, though delays and closures are common.

    Carry your passport, and expect security checks.

Getting around

The Old City is on foot, and the rest of Hebron is served by taxis.

  • Walking with a guide

    The Old City's lanes are narrow and some are closed. A guide can show you the open routes.

    Do not go alone.

  • Taxi

    Taxis are cheap, and can take you to the workshops and the Abraham's Oak area.

    Agree the fare first.

  • Service taxi

    Shared vans link Hebron with Bethlehem, Ramallah and villages.

    Ask locals where to catch them.

When to go

Best time to visit Hebron

Hebron is in the hills at about 930 m and has a Mediterranean climate. Spring and autumn are the best seasons, and the grape harvest in late summer is a highlight.

  • Spring (March–May)

    Best time

    Mild and green, with pleasant days.

  • Summer (June–August)

    Shoulder

    Warm and dry, with the grape harvest in August and September.

  • Autumn (September–November)

    Best time

    Warm and clear, with fewer visitors.

  • Winter (December–February)

    Quiet

    Cold, wet and occasionally snowy. Some routes may close.

Worth planning around: Fridays, when the mosque is busy and access is restricted, and Jewish and Muslim holidays, when the Ibrahimi Mosque is closed to one or other group.

Itinerary

One day in Hebron

A guided visit to the Old City and the Ibrahimi Mosque, lunch at a local restaurant, and a glass workshop. Assumes a day trip from Bethlehem or Jerusalem with a guide.

  1. Day1

    The Old City and the shrine

    History, craft and context.

    • Morning A guided walk through the Old City and the Ibrahimi Mosque.
    • Afternoon Lunch in H1, then a glass or pottery workshop.
    • Evening Return to Bethlehem or Jerusalem before dark.

Travel tips

Hebron travel tips

Hebron requires more care than any other place in this guide.

  • Read your government's advice before you go. Some governments advise against travel to Hebron, and conditions can change at short notice.
  • Go with a local guide. It is the safest and most informative way to see the city, and it helps with access.
  • Carry your passport, and be prepared for security checks and delays.
  • Do not photograph soldiers, checkpoints or military installations, and be careful with photographs of people.
  • Dress modestly, particularly for the mosque, and behave respectfully.
  • Hear more than one perspective. Palestinian, Israeli and international guides give different accounts of the city.
  • Leave before dark, and avoid Fridays and holidays.

FAQ

Hebron: frequently asked questions

Is it safe to visit Hebron?

Hebron is one of the more tense places in the West Bank. Many visitors go without incident, particularly on guided tours, but there have been periods of violence, and some governments advise against travel there. Check current advice and go with a local guide.

What is the Ibrahimi Mosque?

A stone enclosure built by Herod over the cave traditionally regarded as the burial place of Abraham, Isaac, Jacob and their wives. It is holy to Muslims, Jews and Christians, and is divided between a mosque and a synagogue.

Why is Hebron divided?

After a 1994 massacre at the Ibrahimi Mosque, and under the 1997 Hebron Protocol, the city was divided into H1 under Palestinian Authority control and H2, including the Old City, under Israeli control. A small number of Israeli settlers live in H2, protected by the military.

Should I go with a Palestinian or an Israeli guide?

They give different perspectives on the same streets. Many visitors choose one, and some do both. Whichever you choose, ask questions, and read more widely.

How long do you need in Hebron?

Half a day covers the Old City, the mosque and a workshop. A full day lets you add lunch and Abraham's Oak.

Can I visit Hebron from Jerusalem or Bethlehem?

Yes. It is 35 km south of Jerusalem and about 45 minutes by road, though checkpoints add time. Take your passport and a guide.
