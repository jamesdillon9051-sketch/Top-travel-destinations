Why go to New Zealand

New Zealand packs a genuinely improbable range of landscapes into two main islands roughly the size of the United Kingdom — glaciers descending almost to rainforest, geothermal fields that steam and bubble beside working towns, fjords carved by ice into vertical cliffs, and volcanic alpine crossings that feel like walking on another planet.

That geological drama is inseparable from a living Maori culture with a genuinely unbroken presence stretching back centuries before European arrival — Te Reo Maori place names cover the map, and a hangi feast or haka performance is a real cultural encounter, not a staged tourist show, in the places that do it properly.

The country divides cleanly into two islands with distinct characters: the North Island holds most of the population, the geothermal fields and Maori cultural centres, while the South Island holds the more dramatic mountain, fjord and glacier scenery that dominates New Zealand's international image. A well-organised road network and domestic flights make combining both islands in one trip genuinely straightforward, though the distances involved reward building in more driving time than the map alone suggests.

Experiences

Best things to do in New Zealand

The experiences that recur across the country and that make a New Zealand trip feel like more than a checklist of dramatic landscapes.

  1. See a Maori cultural performance

    A genuine haka performance and cultural evening, particularly around Rotorua, offers real insight into Maori history and tradition rather than a staged photo opportunity.

    Tip Seek out a marae-based or iwi-run cultural experience over a generic hotel dinner show for a genuinely more authentic encounter.

  2. Try an adventure sport

    Queenstown genuinely invented commercial bungee jumping at the Kawarau Gorge Bridge in 1988, and the wider region remains the world's most concentrated hub for skydiving, jet boating and canyon swings.

    Tip Book adventure activities a few days ahead in peak summer — the most popular operators genuinely sell out.

  3. Hike the Tongariro Alpine Crossing

    Widely considered one of the world's best day hikes, the crossing genuinely traverses active volcanic terrain, emerald crater lakes and stark alpine desert in a single demanding day.

    Tip Check current volcanic activity alerts before setting out, and book a shuttle in advance since the trail is one-way.

  4. Soak in Rotorua's geothermal springs

    Rotorua's genuinely active geothermal field means natural hot pools, bubbling mud and sulphur-scented steam rising from parks and streets throughout the town.

    Tip A dedicated thermal spa offers a more comfortable soak than the free public pools, which can run uncomfortably hot.

  5. Spot New Zealand's iconic wildlife

    The nocturnal, flightless kiwi is genuinely New Zealand's national symbol, rarely seen in the wild but reliably spotted at dedicated conservation centres, alongside fur seals, dolphins and whales around the coast.

    Tip A guided nocturnal kiwi encounter at a conservation centre is genuinely the most reliable way to see one — wild sightings are rare.

  6. Visit Hobbiton

    The genuine, permanently maintained film set from The Lord of the Rings and The Hobbit trilogies, its hobbit holes and gardens tended year-round rather than left as a temporary prop.

    Tip Book tickets online ahead — access is by guided tour only and popular time slots genuinely sell out.

Food and drink

New Zealand foods to try

New Zealand food draws on genuinely excellent local produce, Maori tradition and a strong café culture found at every price point.

  • Hangi

    A traditional Maori method of cooking meat and vegetables in a pit oven heated by hot stones, genuinely producing a distinctive smoky, tender result found at cultural experiences nationwide.

    Where to try it Maori cultural centres, particularly around Rotorua, offering a genuine hangi feast as part of an evening experience.

  • Pavlova

    A meringue-based dessert with a genuinely crisp shell and marshmallow-soft centre, topped with whipped cream and fresh fruit — a beloved national dessert, its exact origin still debated with Australia.

    Where to try it Cafés and home kitchens nationwide, especially at Christmas and summer gatherings.

  • Fish and chips

    A genuinely beloved national tradition, usually made with fresh local snapper or tarakihi, eaten straight from the paper on a beach or waterfront.

    Where to try it Independent fish and chip shops nationwide, especially good near any coastal town.

  • Meat pie

    A genuinely serious national obsession — flaky pastry filled with steak, mince or a range of other fillings, sold at every bakery and petrol station nationwide.

    Where to try it Independent bakeries nationwide; a good pie is a genuine point of local pride in every town.

  • Green-lipped mussels

    A genuinely distinctive New Zealand species, farmed extensively in the Marlborough Sounds and served simply steamed or in a range of sauces.

    Where to try it Seafood restaurants nationwide, especially good fresh in Picton and the Marlborough region.

  • Hokey pokey ice cream

    Vanilla ice cream studded with pieces of honeycomb toffee, genuinely one of New Zealand's most distinctive and beloved local ice cream flavours.

    Where to try it Ice cream parlours and supermarkets nationwide — a genuine Kiwi childhood staple.

When to go

Best time to visit New Zealand

New Zealand's seasons run opposite the Northern Hemisphere's, and the specific window matters more here than in most destinations because of how weather-dependent many activities are.

  • Summer (December–February)

    Best time

    The warmest, driest weather and the longest days, ideal for hiking, beaches and the Great Walks — also the busiest and most expensive season.

  • Autumn (March–May)

    Best time

    Mild, settled weather with genuinely spectacular autumn colour in Central Otago, and noticeably thinner crowds than summer.

  • Winter (June–August)

    Good

    Cold, especially in the South Island, but genuinely excellent for skiing around Queenstown and Wanaka, with the North Island staying milder.

  • Spring (September–November)

    Good

    Increasingly mild with lambing season in full swing, though mountain weather remains genuinely unpredictable this early in the season.

Worth planning around: Book Great Walks huts, Milford Sound cruises and Queenstown accommodation many months ahead for the December–February peak — this is genuinely the busiest and most tightly booked window of the year.

Trip length

How long to spend in New Zealand

New Zealand's genuinely long driving distances mean a rushed itinerary does the country a disservice — this is not a place to try to see everything in a week.

  • 7–8 days

    One island only — either the North Island's cities and geothermal fields, or the South Island's mountains and fjords.

    Trying to cover both islands in under 10 days genuinely means too much time driving and not enough time anywhere.

  • 12–14 days

    A South Island loop covering Christchurch, Queenstown, Milford Sound and the West Coast glaciers, the most popular single-island itinerary.

    Add a few days in Wanaka or Abel Tasman if the pace allows.

  • 18–21 days

    The itinerary on this page: Auckland, Rotorua, Wellington across the North Island, then Christchurch, Queenstown, Milford Sound and the glaciers across the South.

    A domestic flight between the islands, rather than the ferry, saves genuinely significant time on a trip this length.

  • 4 weeks or more

    Add the Bay of Islands, Tongariro and Abel Tasman in full, all genuinely worth dedicated time rather than a rushed add-on.

    A longer trip is where New Zealand's driving distances stop feeling like a constraint and start feeling like the point.

Getting around

Transportation in New Zealand

New Zealand rewards a rental car more than almost any other destination in this guide — public transport between towns is genuinely limited.

  • Rental car

    Genuinely the standard way to see New Zealand — distances between Queenstown, Milford Sound and the glaciers are far longer than they look on a map.

    Book well ahead in summer, and budget considerably more driving time than a map suggests for winding mountain roads.

  • Domestic flights

    Connect Auckland, Wellington, Christchurch and Queenstown quickly, genuinely worth it for a trip covering both islands in under three weeks.

    Book ahead for the best fares — routes between the main hubs run frequently.

  • Interislander ferry

    Connects Wellington to Picton across Cook Strait, a genuinely scenic crossing through the Marlborough Sounds.

    Book vehicle space well ahead in summer — sailings can sell out.

  • Long-distance buses

    InterCity coaches connect most towns nationwide, a workable budget option for visitors without a car.

    Considerably slower than driving, but a genuinely functional way to see the country on a tighter budget.

  • Guided tours

    Multi-day coach tours cover the country's highlights without needing to drive yourself.

    A genuinely good option for visitors uncomfortable with unfamiliar mountain roads or long driving days.

Culture

Culture and etiquette in New Zealand

New Zealand culture is shaped by a genuine, living Maori heritage alongside a relaxed, outdoorsy national character, with a handful of broadly shared norms holding nationwide.

  • Maori culture deserves genuine respect

    Learning a few Te Reo Maori words, and approaching cultural experiences like a hangi or marae visit with real openness, is genuinely appreciated.

  • A relaxed, first-name culture is the norm

    New Zealanders are genuinely informal and friendly by nature — a casual, direct manner is standard rather than a sign of rudeness.

  • Take mountain and coastal safety seriously

    Weather genuinely changes fast in alpine and coastal areas — check forecasts and conditions before any hike or water activity.

  • Tipping isn't expected

    Restaurant and service prices already reflect fair wages — leaving a tip is genuinely unusual rather than customary.

  • Respect biosecurity rules

    New Zealand's border controls on food, plants and outdoor equipment are genuinely strict — declare everything and clean hiking boots before arrival.

  • BYO and shared plates are common dining customs

    Many restaurants genuinely allow you to bring your own wine (BYO) for a small corkage fee — worth asking about.

Do

  • Learn a few Te Reo Maori words and place names — genuinely appreciated as a sign of respect.
  • Book Great Walk huts, Milford Sound cruises and Queenstown accommodation well ahead in the summer peak.
  • Check mountain and coastal weather forecasts before any hike or water activity — conditions genuinely change fast.
  • Declare all food, plants and used outdoor gear at the border — biosecurity checks are genuinely strict.

Don't

  • Don't summit Ngauruhoe or Tongariro out of respect for their sacred status to Ngati Tuwharetoa, even where technically allowed.
  • Don't expect to tip at restaurants or cafes — it's genuinely unusual rather than customary.
  • Don't underestimate South Island driving times — winding mountain roads genuinely take longer than a map suggests.
  • Don't skip travel insurance with hiking and adventure-sport cover — conditions and activities here carry genuine real risk.

FAQ

Visiting New Zealand: frequently asked questions

How many days do you need in New Zealand?

Two weeks covers one island properly. Eighteen days to three weeks allows the fuller North-and-South itinerary in this guide, with time for the Great Walks, glaciers and fiords without constant rushing.

Do you need a car in New Zealand?

Genuinely, yes, for most itineraries. Public transport between towns is limited, and a rental car gives the flexibility needed to reach Milford Sound, the glaciers and smaller towns on your own schedule.

Is New Zealand expensive to visit?

Moderately to considerably expensive by global standards, comparable to Australia, with accommodation and rental cars the biggest costs. Camping, hostels and cooking your own food bring costs down significantly.

When is the best time to visit New Zealand?

December to February (summer) offers the most reliable weather for hiking and the Great Walks, though it's also the busiest and most expensive. March to May offers a genuinely excellent shoulder-season alternative with thinner crowds.

Should I visit the North Island or South Island?

The South Island holds the more dramatic mountain, fjord and glacier scenery that dominates New Zealand's international image; the North Island holds most of the population, the geothermal fields and the strongest Maori cultural experiences. A first trip of two weeks or more genuinely benefits from covering both.

Do I need a visa for New Zealand?

Most visitors need an NZeTA (New Zealand Electronic Travel Authority), applied for online before arrival, even from countries with visa-free access — apply at least 72 hours ahead of your flight.
