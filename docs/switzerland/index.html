Why go to Switzerland

Switzerland is a small, landlocked country of about 9 million people at the heart of Europe, about the same area as the Netherlands. About 60 percent of its territory is Alps, and the rest is a plateau of lakes, farmland and old cities. It has four national languages, German, French, Italian and Romansh, and a political system built on local cantons that has kept it neutral, stable and rich for centuries.

For visitors, the scenery is the star. The Matterhorn above Zermatt, the Eiger, Mönch and Jungfrau above Grindelwald and Lauterbrunnen, the glaciers of the Aletsch region, the turquoise lakes of Lucerne, Geneva and Lugano, and the vineyards of Lavaux are among the most photographed places in Europe. Just as impressive is how easy it all is to reach: trains, boats, buses and cable cars connect even remote villages on strict timetables, and a single pass covers much of the network.

The other side is cost. Switzerland is one of the most expensive countries in the world, and food, hotels and mountain lifts can shock first-time visitors. With planning, using rail passes, picnicking from supermarkets, staying in hostels or smaller towns and choosing a few big-ticket excursions rather than all of them, it is possible to see the country at a sensible price.

Experiences

Best things to do in Switzerland

The experiences that recur across the country and make a Swiss trip feel like one.

  1. Ride a scenic train

    The Glacier Express, Bernina Express and GoldenPass lines run through panoramic scenery on some of the world's most famous railways. See St. Moritz and the Engadin.

    Tip Book seat reservations early in summer.

  2. Go up a mountain

    Cable cars and cog railways climb to Jungfraujoch, Gornergrat, Pilatus, Rigi and Säntis, and views come with restaurants at the top. See the Jungfrau region and Zermatt.

    Tip Check the webcam before buying a ticket.

  3. See a glacier

    The Aletsch Glacier, the longest in the Alps, is a UNESCO World Heritage site, and Jungfraujoch has ice palaces at 3,454 metres.

    Tip Glaciers are shrinking, so go sooner rather than later.

  4. Hike in the Alps

    Well-marked trails and mountain huts make hiking easy at any level, from lake walks to high passes. The yellow signs give walking times.

    Tip Go between late June and September for open high trails.

  5. Cruise a lake

    Paddle steamers and boats on Lake Lucerne, Lake Geneva, Lake Lugano and Lake Brienz connect towns and castles. See Lucerne and Lake Geneva.

    Tip Boats are included in the Swiss Travel Pass.

  6. Eat cheese and chocolate

    Fondue, raclette and Alpine cheeses in the mountains, and chocolate from Swiss makers. See the food section below.

    Tip Try cheese at a mountain hut.

  7. Wander an old town

    Bern's medieval arcades, Lucerne's wooden bridge and Zurich's guild houses are some of Europe's best preserved old towns. See Bern and Zurich.

    Tip Walk early, before the day trippers arrive.

Food and drink

Switzerland foods to try

Swiss food is built on cheese, potatoes, bread and meat, in French, German and Italian variations, with excellent chocolate and increasingly good wine.

  • Cheese fondue

    Melted Gruyère and Vacherin cheese with white wine, eaten by dipping cubes of bread into a shared pot. It is a winter dish, though it is served year round for visitors.

    Where to try it Mountain restaurants and old-town taverns.

  • Raclette

    Cheese melted and scraped over boiled potatoes, pickles and onions, a favourite in the Valais and at Christmas markets.

    Where to try it Zermatt and Valais restaurants.

  • Rösti

    Grated potato fried into a golden cake, a national dish that comes plain or topped with cheese, bacon or egg.

    Where to try it Restaurants in Bern and across the German-speaking regions.

  • Zürcher Geschnetzeltes

    Sliced veal in a creamy white wine and mushroom sauce, usually served with rösti.

    Where to try it Traditional restaurants in Zurich.

  • Swiss chocolate

    Smooth milk and dark chocolate from Swiss makers, from supermarket bars to hand-made pralines.

    Where to try it Chocolatiers in Zurich, Geneva and Lucerne.

  • Swiss wine

    Chasselas from Lavaux, Pinot Noir from Valais and Merlot from Ticino, most of it drunk in Switzerland rather than exported.

    Where to try it Wine terraces in Lavaux and Ticino.

Before you go

Practical travel information for Switzerland

The essentials, current for 2026. Switzerland is easy and safe, though the prices, the rules and the timetables can catch visitors out.

Capital
Bern (the federal city)
Language
Four national languages: German (about two-thirds), French, Italian and Romansh. Swiss German is spoken in the north and east. English is widely spoken in tourism and cities.
Currency
Swiss franc (CHF). Roughly 0.8–0.9 francs to the US dollar in recent years. Switzerland is not in the eurozone, although euros are accepted in some tourist places at poor rates.
Cards and cash
Cards and contactless payment are accepted almost everywhere, though a few mountain huts and small businesses prefer cash. ATMs are common. Carry some francs for huts and small shops.
Entry and visas
Switzerland is in the Schengen Area but not the EU. Many nationalities, including the United Kingdom, United States, Canada and Australia, can visit for up to 90 days in any 180 without a visa. The EU's ETIAS travel authorisation has been planned for Schengen countries, so check the current rules before you go.
Power sockets
Type J (three round pins), 230 V, 50 Hz. Type C two-pin plugs also fit in many sockets, but an adaptor is safest for UK and US visitors.
Emergency number
112 for all emergencies, 117 for police, 118 for fire, 144 for ambulance, and 1414 for Rega air rescue.
Tipping
Service is included in restaurant prices. Rounding up the bill or leaving a few francs for good service is normal, but not required.
Safety
Switzerland is one of the safest countries in the world for crime. The main risks are in the mountains: weather can change fast, and avalanche, rockfall and altitude are real hazards. Follow trail signs and closures, carry warm clothes and check forecasts. Pickpocketing happens in stations and tourist areas.
Internet and SIM
Excellent, including on mountain tops and trains. Roaming is expensive outside the EU, so buy a local eSIM or a prepaid SIM. SBB Mobile is essential for train timetables and tickets.
Time zone
Central European Time, UTC+1, and UTC+2 in summer (daylight saving time).

When to go

Best time to visit Switzerland

Switzerland has two peak seasons: summer for hiking, lakes and cities, and winter for skiing. Spring and autumn are quieter, with some mountain lifts closed.

  • Spring (April–May)

    Quiet

    Wildflowers and blossom in the valleys, but snow at height and some lifts closed until late May or June.

  • Summer (June–August)

    Best time

    The best time for hiking, lakes and high-mountain excursions, with long days. Crowds and prices peak, and thunderstorms are possible.

  • Autumn (September–October)

    Best time

    Clear air, golden larches and grape harvests, with fewer crowds. Cattle come down from the high pastures in September.

  • Winter (December–March)

    Good

    Skiing and snowy villages, plus Christmas markets. Some hiking routes close, and days are short.

Worth planning around: Late April to mid-May and November, when many mountain lifts and hotels close for maintenance.

Trip length

How long to spend in Switzerland

Switzerland is small and superbly connected, so a week gets you to several regions. Two weeks allows a proper mix of cities, lakes and peaks at a comfortable pace.

Getting around

Transportation in Switzerland

Public transport is superb, with trains, buses, boats and cable cars linked on one timetable. A car is not needed for most trips.

  • Trains (SBB)

    Frequent and punctual, linking every city and most towns. Scenic lines such as the Glacier Express and Bernina Express run through mountains.

    Use the SBB Mobile app for timetables and tickets.

  • Swiss Travel Pass

    Unlimited travel on trains, buses and boats, plus free entry to many museums and discounts on mountain railways. Available for 3 to 15 days.

    Do the sums first. It pays off if you travel a lot.

  • Half Fare Card

    Half-price tickets on most transport for a month.

    Good for a longer stay.

  • PostBuses

    Yellow buses reach mountain villages and passes.

    Check the timetables.

  • Hire cars

    Useful for Ticino, the Engadin and remote valleys. Motorways need a vignette, and parking in towns is expensive.

    Many car-free villages need a train.

Culture

Culture and etiquette in Switzerland

Swiss society values punctuality, order, privacy and respect for rules and nature. People are polite and reserved, and greetings and quiet matter. The country's regions differ, from German-speaking Zurich to French-speaking Geneva and Italian-speaking Lugano.

  • Be on time

    Trains and appointments run on time, and lateness is rude.

  • Greet people

    Say hello (Grüezi in German, Bonjour in French, Buongiorno in Italian) in shops, on trails and to neighbours.

  • Keep quiet

    Speak softly on trains and in hotels, and respect Sunday and evening quiet hours, since laundry and lawn-mowing are avoided.

  • Respect nature

    Stay on trails, close gates, take litter home and do not pick flowers.

  • Recycle

    Sort your rubbish. Rules are strict, and some towns need official bags.

  • Keep to the right

    On escalators and paths, stand on the right and let others pass.

Do

  • Learn a few words in the local language
  • Validate or buy tickets before you board
  • Book scenic trains and mountain excursions early
  • Carry a warm layer even in summer
  • Buy picnic food in supermarkets such as Coop and Migros
  • Drink from public fountains, since the tap water is excellent

Don't

  • Don't be late
  • Don't leave the trail, or approach cows with calves
  • Don't fly a drone without checking the rules
  • Don't make noise late at night or on Sundays
  • Don't feed wildlife or marmots
  • Don't assume everything is open on Sundays

Travel tips

Switzerland travel tips

The things that most often catch visitors out in Switzerland, several of which are specific to how the country works.

  • Do the sums on the Swiss Travel Pass against point-to-point tickets and regional passes.
  • Check webcams and forecasts before paying for a mountain ticket.
  • Buy picnic food from supermarkets, since restaurants are expensive.
  • Book scenic trains and hotels far ahead for July and August.
  • Stay in smaller towns such as Lucerne or Thun and day-trip to save on hotels.
  • Bring a warm layer and sun protection, since mountains are cold and bright.
  • Note that shops close on Sundays in many towns.
  • Use the SBB Mobile app for timetables and ticketing.

FAQ

Visiting Switzerland: frequently asked questions

Is Switzerland expensive?

Yes. It is one of the world's most expensive countries. Supermarket picnics, hostels, regional passes and choosing fewer mountain excursions help a lot.

How many days do you need in Switzerland?

Eight to fourteen days for a first trip. A week covers Zurich, Lucerne and the Jungfrau.

When is the best time to visit Switzerland?

June to September for hiking and lakes, and December to March for skiing. September is often ideal.

Is the Swiss Travel Pass worth it?

It suits fast-moving trips with lots of trains, boats and museum visits. For a slower trip, regional passes or a Half Fare Card may be cheaper.

Do I need a car?

No. Public transport is superb. A car helps only in Ticino and remote valleys.

Do I need a visa for Switzerland?

Many nationalities can visit visa-free for 90 days under Schengen rules. Check current entry requirements, including any ETIAS changes.
