How to plan an Ireland trip: whether to focus on Dublin, the southwest or the west coast, when to go for the best weather odds, what it costs, and a practical nine-day itinerary.

How Ireland divides

Ireland genuinely divides into a compact set of regions, each with a distinct feel — its small size means a first trip can realistically cover several without feeling rushed.

The east. Dublin and Kilkenny — the capital's museums and pubs, and a genuinely well-preserved medieval town within easy reach.

The southwest. Cork and Cobh, Killarney and the Ring of Kerry and the Dingle Peninsula — Ireland's most famous scenic driving country, and genuinely the reason many people visit.

The west. the Cliffs of Moher and the Burren, Galway and Connemara — dramatic Atlantic coastline and a genuinely lively university city.

The northwest and islands. Donegal and the Aran Islands are both genuinely spectacular but sit further off the classic first-timer route.

Worth knowing: Most first-time visitors genuinely combine Dublin with the southwest and west coasts in a single loop. Donegal and the Aran Islands are where a longer or second trip belongs.

Choosing between the ten destinations

  • If this is your first trip

    Dublin, genuinely the natural starting point, with world-class museums and the best transport links.

  • If you want the classic scenic drives

    Killarney and the Ring of Kerry plus the Dingle Peninsula, genuinely Ireland's most celebrated coastal driving country.

  • If you want dramatic cliffs in a shorter trip

    The Cliffs of Moher and the Burren, genuinely reachable as a long day trip from Galway or Dublin.

  • If you want a lively, walkable city

    Galway, genuinely famous for its music-filled streets and easy access to the west coast.

  • If you want real remoteness

    The Aran Islands, genuinely still Irish-speaking, reachable only by ferry or small plane.

  • If you want the far northwest's wild cliffs

    Donegal, home to Slieve League, genuinely among Europe's highest sea cliffs and far quieter than the southwest.

Getting around: driving, trains, and one common mistake

Ireland's small size genuinely disguises how slow its roads actually are — a mix of rental car for the coast and train for the main towns is the standard approach.

Rent a car for the west and southwest. It's genuinely the most flexible way to see the Ring of Kerry, Dingle and Connemara at your own pace.

Don't underestimate driving times. A route that looks like ninety minutes on a map can genuinely take three hours on narrow, winding coast roads.

Take the train between the bigger towns. Dublin, Cork, Galway and Kilkenny are genuinely well connected without a car.

Book island ferries around the weather. Sailings to the Aran Islands genuinely depend on conditions and can be cancelled at short notice.

Drive on the left, and take single-track roads slowly. Rural roads are genuinely narrower, and hedges taller, than most visitors expect.

Worth knowing: Book your rental car and west-coast accommodation well ahead for a June–August trip — Ireland's small hotel and guesthouse stock genuinely sells out early in peak season.

When to go

Ireland's weather is famously changeable in every season, so 'the best time' is really about balancing crowds against the odds of a dry day. May, June and September generally work best.

Late spring brings genuinely the best odds of mild, dry weather with long daylight and thinner crowds than midsummer.

Summer is warmest and busiest, with the longest days — also genuinely the most expensive and crowded season, especially along the west coast.

Early autumn is a genuinely underrated window, often mild with noticeably fewer visitors than summer.

Winter brings short days and frequent rain, but is genuinely atmospheric, with cosy pubs, fewer crowds and lower prices.

Worth knowing: Pack a proper waterproof jacket regardless of when you go — a sunny forecast in Ireland genuinely offers no guarantee by the afternoon.

Four routes that work

  1. Four days: Dublin alone

    Dublin's museums, castle and pubs, genuinely enough for a satisfying short city break.

    Tip Add a single day trip to Kilkenny by train if you have a fifth day free.

  2. Nine days: the classic loop

    The itinerary on the Ireland country page: Dublin, Kilkenny, Cork, Killarney, Dingle, the Cliffs of Moher and Galway. A genuinely complete first look at Ireland's classic highlights.

    Tip Break up the longer driving days with an early start — west-coast light is genuinely best in the morning.

  3. A week focused on the west coast

    Galway, Connemara and the Cliffs of Moher as a self-contained trip, skipping the long transit south.

    Tip Base yourself in Galway and take day trips rather than changing accommodation each night.

  4. A Donegal and Aran Islands add-on for a second trip

    Donegal's Slieve League cliffs and the Aran Islands, genuinely two of Ireland's most remote and rewarding corners.

    Tip Rent a bike on Inis Mór rather than joining a minibus tour for a genuinely slower, quieter visit.

FAQ

Planning a trip to Ireland

How many days do you need in Ireland?

Nine to twelve days covers Dublin and a loop through the south and west coast at a reasonable pace, which is the itinerary in this guide. Four to five days is enough for Dublin and one nearby region, and genuinely still worthwhile on its own.

Is the west coast worth the extra driving time?

Yes, genuinely — the Ring of Kerry, Dingle and the Cliffs of Moher are a large part of why people visit, and the slower pace is part of the experience rather than a drawback.

Do you need a car in Ireland?

For the west and southwest coasts, yes, genuinely — though trains and buses cover Dublin, Cork, Galway and Kilkenny well without one.

What is the best time of year to visit for good weather?

May, June and September offer genuinely the best odds of mild, dry weather with thinner crowds than July and August, though no season in Ireland guarantees a dry day.
