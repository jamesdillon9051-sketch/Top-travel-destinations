Welcome to Kyoto

Kyoto served as Japan's imperial capital for over a thousand years, from 794 until the emperor's move to Tokyo in 1868, and the city's more than 1,600 Buddhist temples and 400 Shinto shrines reflect that millennium of continuous religious and cultural investment. Unlike Tokyo, largely rebuilt after 20th-century destruction, Kyoto was deliberately spared major Allied bombing, leaving genuine historic fabric standing throughout the city rather than careful postwar reconstruction.

That preservation extends beyond buildings into living practice — the Gion district's geisha, properly called geiko in Kyoto's own dialect, still train and perform using traditions largely unchanged for centuries, and traditional kaiseki dining, tea ceremony and kimono culture remain genuinely practiced rather than preserved only for tourists.

The city's popularity has real consequences worth planning around: Kyoto now manages some of Japan's most serious overtourism pressure, particularly in Gion and around its most famous temples, making early starts and a willingness to seek out quieter corners genuinely worthwhile.

Why visit

Why visit Kyoto?

Kyoto's case is that no other Japanese city holds this much genuine, continuously practiced tradition at this scale.

  • The temple density is genuinely unmatched

    Over 1,600 Buddhist temples and 400 Shinto shrines give Kyoto a concentration of historic religious sites no other Japanese city approaches.

  • The traditions are genuinely still practiced

    Geiko culture, tea ceremony and kaiseki dining continue as living practice, not preservation for its own sake.

  • The historic fabric genuinely survived

    Spared major wartime bombing, Kyoto's old streets and buildings are authentically old rather than rebuilt.

  • The gardens are genuinely world class

    From Zen rock gardens to Arashiyama's bamboo grove, Kyoto's landscaped spaces set the standard for Japanese garden design.

  • It works well as a base for the wider Kansai region

    Nara, Osaka and Uji all sit within easy day-trip reach for those with more time.

Main attractions

Top things to see in Kyoto

Kyoto's essential sights, spanning its most famous shrine, its landmark temples and its preserved geisha district.

  1. 01

    Fushimi Inari Shrine

    Thousands of vermillion torii gates climb the mountainside behind the shrine, forming a genuinely otherworldly tunnel, dedicated to Inari, the Shinto deity of rice and prosperity.

    Tip Walk past the first busy stretch near the entrance — the crowds thin noticeably within twenty minutes further up the mountain.

  2. 02

    Kinkaku-ji, the Golden Pavilion

    A Zen temple's top two floors covered entirely in gold leaf, reflected in the still pond before it, one of Japan's most photographed buildings.

    Tip Arrive at opening time to see the pavilion's reflection before wind or crowds disturb the pond's surface.

  3. 03

    Kiyomizu-dera

    A wooden temple built without a single nail, its famous stage jutting out over the hillside on tall pillars, with genuinely sweeping views across the city.

    Tip The approach streets, Sannen-zaka and Ninen-zaka, are genuinely worth the walk in their own right, lined with traditional shops.

  4. 04

    Arashiyama Bamboo Grove

    A genuinely striking path through towering bamboo stalks, the light filtering green overhead, on the city's western edge alongside temples and a scenic river.

    Tip Visit at first light — the grove becomes extremely crowded by mid-morning and loses much of its atmosphere.

  5. 05

    Gion district

    Kyoto's famous geisha district, its preserved wooden machiya townhouses and narrow lanes offering a genuine, if increasingly managed, glimpse of traditional entertainment culture.

    Tip Photographing geiko or maiko directly without consent is now restricted on some private streets — respect posted signage.

  6. 06

    Nijo Castle

    A shogun's former residence with genuinely deliberate 'nightingale floors' designed to chirp underfoot as a defence against silent intruders, set within landscaped grounds.

    Tip Listen for the floor's distinctive squeak as you walk — it is a genuine 17th-century security feature, not wear and tear.

Things to do

Best things to do in Kyoto

Beyond the temples and shrines, Kyoto rewards its living traditions and the quieter corners beyond the main sights.

  1. Wear a kimono for a day

    Rental shops across the city fit visitors with kimono and accessories for a day of temple-hopping in genuinely fitting historic dress.

    Tip Book ahead in cherry blossom or autumn foliage season, when rental shops sell out their most popular styles early.

  2. Experience a traditional tea ceremony

    A formal or informal tea ceremony session offers real insight into the precision and philosophy behind Japan's tea culture, beyond simply drinking matcha.

    Tip Shorter, English-language sessions aimed at visitors are widely available and still genuinely worthwhile, not a diluted experience.

  3. Eat your way through Nishiki Market

    A narrow covered market street of small food stalls, dubbed 'Kyoto's kitchen', offering genuine local specialities in bite-sized, easy-to-sample portions.

    Tip Eating while walking through the market itself is generally discouraged — many stalls have small standing areas for this reason.

  4. Walk the Philosopher's Path

    A genuinely peaceful canal-side path between Ginkaku-ji and Nanzen-ji temples, named for a philosophy professor who walked it daily, lined with cherry trees.

    Tip Early morning or early evening gives the path a genuinely quieter, more contemplative atmosphere than midday.

  5. Day trip to Uji for matcha

    A short train ride from Kyoto, Uji is Japan's historic centre of matcha cultivation, with genuinely serious tea houses and the UNESCO-listed Byodo-in temple.

    Tip Byodo-in's image appears on the Japanese ten-yen coin — worth comparing once you're standing in front of it.

  6. Stay a night in a traditional ryokan

    A traditional inn with tatami floors, futon bedding and a multi-course kaiseki dinner offers genuine insight into Japanese hospitality beyond a standard hotel stay.

    Tip Book directly with smaller family-run ryokan for a more personal experience than the larger, more hotel-like chains.

Local food

What to eat in Kyoto

Kyoto's food culture centres on refined, seasonal cooking, shaped by centuries of imperial and temple tradition.

  • Kaiseki

    A multi-course traditional meal built around seasonal ingredients and careful presentation, Kyoto is genuinely considered kaiseki's spiritual and historic home.

    Where to try it Traditional ryokan inns and dedicated kaiseki restaurants across the city, particularly in the Gion and Pontocho districts.

  • Yudofu

    Simple simmered tofu in a light broth, a genuine Buddhist temple food tradition, particularly associated with the temples around Arashiyama.

    Where to try it Temple-adjacent restaurants around Arashiyama and Nanzen-ji, some centuries-old.

  • Matcha and wagashi

    Whisked green tea alongside delicate seasonal wagashi confections, a genuine tea-ceremony tradition that runs deep in Kyoto's everyday café culture too.

    Where to try it Traditional tea houses in Gion and around Uji, alongside dedicated matcha cafés citywide.

  • Obanzai home cooking

    Kyoto's own tradition of simple, seasonal home-style dishes, historically built around using every part of an ingredient without waste.

    Where to try it Small obanzai restaurants throughout the city, particularly around Nishiki Market.

  • Kyoto-style soba and udon

    Noodles served in a genuinely lighter, more delicate broth than Tokyo's own darker style, reflecting Kansai cuisine's broader preference for subtlety.

    Where to try it Traditional noodle restaurants citywide, many generations-old family businesses.

  • Fushimi sake

    Kyoto's Fushimi district is one of Japan's most historically significant sake-brewing regions, its water genuinely prized for centuries by brewers.

    Where to try it Sake breweries and tasting rooms in the Fushimi district, several open for tours.

Transport

Getting to and around Kyoto

Kyoto has no major airport of its own, reached instead via Osaka or by shinkansen from elsewhere in Japan.

Getting there

  • Kansai International Airport (KIX)

    The Haruka express train reaches Kyoto Station in about 75 minutes, the main international gateway for the wider Kansai region.

    Book the Haruka's discounted foreign-visitor fare online ahead for a genuine saving over the walk-up price.

  • Shinkansen from other Japanese cities

    Bullet trains connect Kyoto to Tokyo in about 2h15 and to Osaka in around 15 minutes.

    Kyoto Station sits directly on the Tokaido Shinkansen line, making it an easy stop on almost any Japan itinerary.

  • Rental car

    Genuinely unnecessary and often impractical within the city — public transport and walking cover Kyoto's sights comfortably.

    Consider a car only for reaching more remote countryside temples well outside the main tourist circuit.

Getting around

Kyoto's bus network covers the temple circuit extensively, alongside a smaller but useful subway system.

  • City buses

    A dense network reaches nearly every major temple and shrine, usable with a Suica or Pasmo IC card or a one-day bus pass.

    Buses can be genuinely crowded and slow in peak season — build extra time into any bus-dependent plan.

  • Walking and cycling

    Central districts like Gion and Higashiyama are genuinely walkable, and bicycle rental is a popular, efficient way to cover more ground.

    Many streets in the old districts are narrow and pedestrian-heavy — cycle carefully around temple approaches.

  • Subway and rail

    Faster than buses for longer crosstown journeys, and the Randen tram line is a genuinely scenic way to reach Arashiyama.

    Combine subway and bus one-day passes for the best value on a temple-heavy sightseeing day.

When to go

Best time to visit Kyoto

Kyoto's temple gardens are genuinely built around seasonal change, and spring and autumn showcase the city at its most photogenic.

  • Spring (late March–May)

    Best time

    Cherry blossoms in late March into April draw Kyoto's biggest crowds of the year, for genuinely spectacular reason.

  • Summer (June–August)

    Shoulder

    Hot, humid and prone to a June rainy season, though also home to the genuinely spectacular Gion Matsuri festival in July.

  • Autumn (November)

    Best time

    Spectacular koyo foliage across the city's temple gardens, arguably rivalling spring blossoms for spectacle with slightly different crowds.

  • Winter (December–February)

    Good

    Cold but often clear, with genuinely peaceful temple visits and occasional snow dusting Kinkaku-ji's gold pavilion beautifully.

Worth planning around: Peak cherry blossom weekends without accommodation booked many months ahead, when Kyoto's most famous sights become genuinely difficult to enjoy at a relaxed pace.

Itinerary

Three days in Kyoto

Eastern temples first, then the golden pavilion and bamboo grove, then the city's living traditions.

  1. Day1

    Higashiyama and Gion

    Kyoto's eastern temple district.

    • Morning Kiyomizu-dera and the Sannen-zaka approach streets.
    • Afternoon Gion district and Yasaka Shrine.
    • Evening Dinner in Pontocho's riverside alley.
  2. Day2

    Fushimi Inari and Nishiki Market

    The torii gates and the city's food culture.

    • Morning Fushimi Inari Shrine, arriving early.
    • Afternoon Nishiki Market food crawl.
    • Evening A traditional kaiseki dinner.
  3. Day3

    Kinkaku-ji and Arashiyama

    The golden pavilion and the bamboo grove.

    • Morning Kinkaku-ji, arriving at opening time.
    • Afternoon Arashiyama's bamboo grove and riverside temples.
    • Evening A tea ceremony or the Philosopher's Path at dusk.

Travel tips

Kyoto travel tips

Practical notes on timing, tickets and etiquette that make a real difference to a Kyoto trip.

  • Visit Fushimi Inari, Kinkaku-ji and the Arashiyama bamboo grove at opening time to genuinely beat the crowds.
  • Book accommodation many months ahead for cherry blossom season and autumn foliage in November.
  • Respect Gion's photography restrictions — some private streets now prohibit photographing geiko and maiko without consent.
  • Get a Suica or Pasmo IC card, or a one-day bus pass, before a temple-heavy sightseeing day.
  • Wear comfortable, easily removable shoes — many temples require shoes off at the entrance.
  • Consider a day trip to Uji or Nara to genuinely reduce Kyoto's peak-season crowding on your remaining days.
  • Book kimono rental and tea ceremony sessions a day or two ahead in peak season.

FAQ

Kyoto: frequently asked questions

How many days do you need in Kyoto?

Three to four. Two covers the essential temples at a rushed pace; four allows day trips to Uji or Nara without feeling hurried.

Is Kyoto very crowded?

Yes, particularly at its most famous sights during cherry blossom season and November's autumn foliage — early starts and weekday visits genuinely help avoid the worst of it.

Is Kyoto worth visiting as a day trip from Osaka?

It works for a single highlight, but the city genuinely rewards at least two full days given its temple density — a rushed day trip covers only a fraction of what makes it worthwhile.

What is the best time of year to visit?

Late March to April for cherry blossoms and November for autumn foliage offer the most spectacular scenery, though both are also the most crowded. Winter offers a quieter, still genuinely beautiful alternative.

Can I see geisha in Kyoto?

Occasionally, particularly around dusk in Gion, though most sightings are brief and increasingly managed by local photography restrictions — a proper teahouse booking offers a more genuine, respectful encounter than street photography.
