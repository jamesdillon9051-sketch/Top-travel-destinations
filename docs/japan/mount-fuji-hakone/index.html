Welcome to Mount Fuji and Hakone

Mount Fuji is Japan's highest peak and one of its most enduring cultural symbols, a genuinely sacred site in both Shinto and Buddhist tradition for centuries, and instantly recognisable well beyond Japan through centuries of woodblock prints and photography. Its famously near-perfect cone shape, formed by relatively recent volcanic activity, is genuinely visible from Tokyo on clear days despite sitting nearly 100 kilometres away.

Hakone, in the mountain's foothills, developed as a hot-spring resort area specifically because of its volcanic geology and its views toward Fuji, and remains one of Japan's most popular onsen destinations for exactly that combination — genuine geothermal bathing culture paired with some of the country's best mountain scenery.

The region works particularly well as an accessible escape from Tokyo, reachable in around two hours, offering lake cruises, volcanic valleys, art museums and traditional ryokan onsen stays without requiring the logistics of a longer regional trip.

Why visit

Why visit Mount Fuji and Hakone?

The pairing's case is that no other easy Tokyo escape combines this iconic a natural landmark with this developed an onsen culture built specifically around it.

  • Mount Fuji is genuinely one of the world's iconic mountains

    Its near-perfect cone and deep cultural significance make it instantly recognisable and genuinely moving to see in person.

  • Hakone's onsen culture is genuinely excellent

    Volcanic hot springs and traditional ryokan inns offer some of the country's best accessible bathing culture.

  • The scenery beyond Fuji itself is genuinely rewarding

    Lake Ashi, the Owakudani volcanic valley and the Hakone Open-Air Museum all stand on their own merits.

  • It's genuinely easy from Tokyo

    Under two hours by train makes this one of the most accessible major nature escapes from the capital.

  • It works well as a short, focused trip

    One or two nights genuinely covers the highlights without needing to build a longer regional itinerary around it.

Main attractions

Top things to see in Mount Fuji and Hakone

Mount Fuji's best viewpoints, paired with Hakone's volcanic valleys and lakeside shrine.

  1. 01

    Mount Fuji from Lake Kawaguchiko

    One of the most reliable and photographed views of the mountain, its snow-capped cone reflected in the lake on still, clear days.

    Tip Early morning generally offers the clearest views, before afternoon cloud cover tends to build around the peak.

  2. 02

    Chureito Pagoda

    A five-storey pagoda on a hillside near Fujiyoshida, framing Mount Fuji behind it in one of Japan's most reproduced single images.

    Tip A short but genuinely steep climb of steps reaches the pagoda — arrive early to beat both crowds and any midday haze.

  3. 03

    Lake Ashi

    A scenic crater lake in Hakone, crossed by a genuinely popular replica pirate ship cruise, with Mount Fuji visible from its shores on clear days.

    Tip The lake's western shore near Hakone Shrine offers the best combined view of the water, the torii gate and the mountain.

  4. 04

    Owakudani volcanic valley

    An active volcanic valley of steaming vents and sulphurous ground, reachable by ropeway, genuinely striking and distinctly different from anywhere else in the region.

    Tip The ropeway occasionally suspends service due to volcanic activity levels — check current status before planning a visit.

  5. 05

    Hakone Shrine's lakeside torii

    A red torii gate standing directly in Lake Ashi's water, a genuinely photogenic complement to Miyajima's more famous floating gate.

    Tip Early morning offers both good light and noticeably thinner crowds at this genuinely popular photo spot.

  6. 06

    Hakone Open-Air Museum

    A genuinely excellent sculpture museum set across landscaped outdoor grounds with mountain views, including a dedicated Picasso pavilion.

    Tip Allow at least two hours — the grounds are larger than they first appear, spread across a genuinely substantial hillside.

Things to do

Best things to do in Mount Fuji and Hakone

Beyond the viewpoints, this region rewards its onsen culture, its lake cruise and its genuinely striking volcanic geology.

  1. Soak in an onsen with a Fuji view

    Several ryokan and public bathhouses in the region offer genuinely rare outdoor baths with a direct mountain view, a signature regional experience.

    Tip Book a ryokan with a private in-room bath (kashikiri) if tattoos are a concern at shared public baths.

  2. Ride the Hakone Ropeway over Owakudani

    A cable car crossing directly above the volcanic valley's steaming vents, offering genuinely dramatic aerial views of the landscape below.

    Tip Sit on the side facing Mount Fuji for the best combined view of the volcano and the valley on a clear day.

  3. Cruise Lake Ashi on the pirate ship

    A genuinely enjoyable, slightly kitsch replica pirate ship crosses the lake between Hakone-machi and Togendai, with mountain views throughout.

    Tip The crossing is short — combine it with a walk along the lake's cedar-lined Old Tokaido Road for a fuller visit.

  4. Eat a black egg at Owakudani

    Eggs boiled in the valley's sulphurous hot springs turn genuinely black, and eating one is said to add seven years to your life.

    Tip Buy them fresh at the Owakudani station shop — they don't keep particularly well beyond the same day.

  5. Climb Mount Fuji, in season

    The official climbing season runs July through early September, when huts and trails are open; outside this window, climbing is genuinely discouraged for safety.

    Tip Book mountain hut accommodation well ahead if attempting an overnight climb to see sunrise from the summit.

  6. Visit the Hakone Open-Air Museum's hot spring foot bath

    A genuinely relaxing onsen foot bath sits within the museum's sculpture grounds, a pleasant way to rest mid-visit.

    Tip Bring a small towel — none are provided, and wet feet make the museum's remaining galleries less comfortable.

Local food

What to eat in Mount Fuji and Hakone

The Fuji and Hakone region's food centres on its volcanic geology, mountain vegetables and Yamanashi's own noodle tradition.

  • Kuro-tamago (black eggs)

    Eggs hard-boiled in Owakudani's sulphurous hot springs, turning their shells genuinely black, a signature regional novelty snack.

    Where to try it The Owakudani station shop, the eggs' genuine place of origin.

  • Hoto noodles

    Thick, flat udon-style noodles simmered in a miso-based vegetable stew, a genuine Yamanashi Prefecture speciality near Mount Fuji's northern side.

    Where to try it Traditional restaurants around Kawaguchiko and the wider Fuji Five Lakes region.

  • Onsen manju

    Steamed buns filled with sweet bean paste, a genuine hot-spring town tradition found throughout Hakone's shopping streets.

    Where to try it Traditional sweet shops throughout Hakone-Yumoto's main street.

  • Yuba

    Delicate tofu skin, served fresh or dried, a genuinely refined regional speciality often featured in kaiseki-style set meals.

    Where to try it Traditional ryokan dinners and dedicated yuba restaurants throughout Hakone.

  • Hakone soba

    Buckwheat noodles served in the region's own style, often featuring local mountain vegetables, a genuinely simple, satisfying regional staple.

    Where to try it Traditional soba restaurants throughout Hakone-Yumoto and along the old Tokaido Road.

  • Sansai mountain vegetables

    Foraged mountain vegetables and wild greens, prepared simply, a genuine seasonal tradition in the mountainous regions around Fuji and Hakone.

    Where to try it Traditional restaurants and ryokan set menus, particularly in spring when foraged varieties are freshest.

Accommodation

Where to stay in Mount Fuji and Hakone

The region's ryokan and onsen inns are as much the destination as any single sight, particularly for a Fuji-view soak.

  • Hakone-Yumoto

    The region's main gateway town, with the widest range of ryokan and easy access to the rest of Hakone.

    Best for: Onsen culture and transport connections

  • Lake Kawaguchiko

    Generally offers clearer, more consistent Mount Fuji views than Hakone itself, with its own onsen and lakeside accommodation.

    Best for: The best reliable Fuji views

  • Gora

    Higher into Hakone's hills, genuinely peaceful, with direct access to the cable car toward Owakudani.

    Best for: A quieter mountain base

Transport

Getting to and around Mount Fuji and Hakone

Both Hakone and the Fuji Five Lakes region sit within easy reach of Tokyo by direct train or bus.

Getting there

  • Train from Tokyo

    The Romancecar limited express reaches Hakone-Yumoto from Shinjuku in about 85 minutes, a genuinely comfortable, reserved-seat direct option.

    The Hakone Freepass covers the Romancecar plus most local transport within the region on one ticket.

  • Highway bus to Kawaguchiko

    Direct buses from Shinjuku reach Lake Kawaguchiko in about 2 hours, the most straightforward route for Fuji-focused visits.

    Book ahead on weekends and during peak leaf-viewing or climbing season, when buses can sell out.

  • Rental car

    Useful for combining both Hakone and the Fuji Five Lakes in one trip at a flexible pace.

    Mountain roads can be narrow and winding — allow more time than GPS estimates suggest.

Getting around

Hakone's own multi-modal transport loop is part of the experience, combining train, cable car, ropeway and boat.

  • The Hakone Freepass loop

    A genuinely well-designed circuit combining a mountain railway, cable car, ropeway and the Lake Ashi pirate ship into one continuous route.

    The full loop takes most of a day if stopping at each major sight along the way.

  • Local buses, around Kawaguchiko

    A dedicated sightseeing bus loop connects the Fuji Five Lakes region's main viewpoints and museums.

    A one or two-day bus pass offers genuine value if visiting several stops around the lake.

  • Rental car

    Offers the most flexibility for reaching quieter viewpoints away from the main sightseeing routes.

    Winter tyres or chains are genuinely necessary on mountain roads in the colder months.

When to go

Best time to visit Mount Fuji and Hakone

Counterintuitively, Mount Fuji is often most visible in the drier, colder months, while Hakone's onsen culture works genuinely well year-round.

  • Spring (March–May)

    Good

    Cherry blossoms frame Fuji beautifully when visible, though cloud cover more often obscures the peak than in winter.

  • Summer (June–August)

    Shoulder

    The only season for climbing Fuji itself, though also the cloudiest and most humid for viewing the mountain from a distance.

  • Autumn (September–November)

    Best time

    Increasingly clear skies as autumn progresses, with genuinely spectacular foliage around Lake Kawaguchiko in November.

  • Winter (December–February)

    Best time

    The clearest, most reliable Fuji views of the year, alongside genuinely excellent cold-weather onsen bathing.

Worth planning around: Peak summer if the mountain view itself is the priority — humidity and cloud cover obscure Fuji far more often than in the cooler, clearer months.

Itinerary

Two days in Mount Fuji and Hakone

A Fuji viewpoint day first, then Hakone's onsen, lake and volcanic valley loop.

  1. Day1

    Mount Fuji viewpoints

    The mountain's best-known angles.

    • Morning Chureito Pagoda, arriving early for clear light.
    • Afternoon Lake Kawaguchiko's shoreline and viewpoints.
    • Evening Check into a ryokan with an onsen bath.
  2. Day2

    The Hakone loop

    Volcanic valley, lake cruise and lakeside shrine.

    • Morning The Hakone Ropeway over Owakudani, with a black egg stop.
    • Afternoon Lake Ashi's pirate ship cruise and Hakone Shrine's torii.
    • Evening A final onsen soak before departure.

Travel tips

Mount Fuji and Hakone travel tips

Practical notes on visibility, timing and transport that make a real difference to a Fuji and Hakone trip.

  • Check current Mount Fuji visibility forecasts — the mountain is genuinely often hidden by cloud, especially in summer.
  • Buy a Hakone Freepass if doing the full loop — it covers most local transport on one ticket at genuine savings.
  • Book ryokan with a Fuji-view onsen well ahead — the best-positioned rooms sell out first, especially on weekends.
  • Only attempt to climb Mount Fuji during the official July–early September season, when huts and safety infrastructure are open.
  • Check the Hakone Ropeway's current operating status before planning around it — volcanic activity occasionally suspends service.
  • Visit Chureito Pagoda and Kawaguchiko viewpoints early morning for the clearest views before afternoon cloud typically builds.
  • Pack layers even in summer — Hakone's elevation makes evenings genuinely cooler than Tokyo.

FAQ

Mount Fuji and Hakone: frequently asked questions

How many days do you need for Mount Fuji and Hakone?

Two works well — one day for Fuji viewpoints around Kawaguchiko, one for Hakone's onsen, lake cruise and volcanic valley loop.

Can I actually see Mount Fuji, or is it often hidden by cloud?

It's genuinely often obscured, especially in summer — winter and clear autumn mornings offer the most reliable views, and checking a same-day forecast is worth doing before committing to a viewpoint.

Do I need to climb Mount Fuji to appreciate the trip?

No — most visitors come for the views and Hakone's onsen culture rather than the climb itself, which is only feasible in the official July to early September season.

What is the best time of year to visit?

Late autumn and winter offer the clearest mountain views, though spring's cherry blossoms and autumn's foliage both add real scenic value when the peak does show.

Is this region easy as a day trip from Tokyo?

Hakone works reasonably as a long day trip, though an overnight ryokan stay with an onsen bath is genuinely worth the extra time if your schedule allows it.
