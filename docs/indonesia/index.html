Why go to Indonesia

Indonesia is genuinely difficult to summarise, because it isn't really one place — it's more than seventeen thousand islands spread across three time zones, home to hundreds of distinct languages and cultures, from Hindu Bali to Islamic Java to the animist highlands of Sumatra and the wild eastern frontier of Papua.

Most visitors genuinely start with Bali, and it earns the attention — rice terraces, surf breaks, temples and a genuinely warm, ritual-rich culture — but Bali is a single small island in a country that also holds an active volcano you can watch erupt from a safe distance, a UNESCO temple complex bigger than most cathedrals, wild Komodo dragons, and reefs in Raja Ampat that marine biologists consider the richest on Earth.

The scale genuinely shapes how you should plan a trip: island-hopping here means real flights and ferries, not quick day trips, so most visitors pick two or three regions rather than trying to cover the whole archipelago in one go.

Experiences

Best things to do in Indonesia

The experiences that recur across the archipelago and that make an Indonesia trip feel like more than a Bali beach holiday.

  1. Watch the sunrise over an active volcano

    Mount Bromo genuinely delivers one of Southeast Asia's great sunrise views, a smoking crater rising out of a sea of mist and volcanic sand.

    Tip Book a jeep the genuinely night before — the viewpoint car park fills fast in the pre-dawn dark.

  2. See wild Komodo dragons

    Komodo and Rinca islands are genuinely the only places on Earth these giant monitor lizards still live wild, best seen with a ranger guide on a short walking trail.

    Tip Rinca genuinely offers a higher dragon-sighting chance than Komodo island itself, with a shorter, easier walk.

  3. Dive or snorkel a world-class reef

    Raja Ampat genuinely holds the richest marine biodiversity recorded anywhere on Earth, though Komodo's reefs and Bali's Amed and Tulamben aren't far behind.

    Tip Raja Ampat's own best visibility window runs roughly October to April, genuinely opposite Bali and Komodo's dry season — check with operators before booking.

  4. Explore an ancient temple complex

    Borobudur, the world's largest Buddhist temple, and nearby Hindu Prambanan are genuinely two of Southeast Asia's great ancient monuments, both UNESCO World Heritage sites.

    Tip A genuinely sunrise entry ticket at Borobudur gets you inside before the day-trip crowds arrive.

  5. Surf a world-class break

    Bali's Bukit Peninsula genuinely holds some of the world's best-known breaks, from Uluwatu's barrels to Canggu's beginner-friendly beach breaks.

    Tip Dry season (April to October) genuinely brings the most consistent swell on Bali's south and west coasts.

  6. Relax on a car-free island

    The Gili Islands off Lombok are genuinely car-free, with horse carts and bicycles the only transport and a laid-back pace to match.

    Tip Gili Trawangan is genuinely the liveliest of the three; Gili Meno is the quietest for an actual break.

Food and drink

Indonesia foods to try

Indonesian food's reputation abroad rarely does justice to a genuinely vast, regionally distinct cuisine spanning thousands of islands.

  • Nasi goreng

    Genuinely Indonesia's unofficial national dish, a smoky fried rice built around sweet soy sauce (kecap manis), usually topped with a fried egg.

    Where to try it Warungs and restaurants nationwide, at every price point.

  • Satay (sate)

    Genuinely ubiquitous grilled meat skewers, usually served with a rich peanut sauce, varying by region from Madura's chicken satay to Bali's minced lilit.

    Where to try it Street carts and warungs nationwide, especially in the evening.

  • Rendang

    A genuinely slow-cooked Minangkabau beef dish from West Sumatra, simmered for hours in coconut milk and spices until deeply rich and dark.

    Where to try it Padang restaurants nationwide, and especially in West Sumatra itself.

  • Gado-gado

    A genuinely satisfying vegetable and boiled-egg salad tossed in peanut sauce, one of Indonesia's most reliable options for vegetarians.

    Where to try it Warungs nationwide, especially in Jakarta and Java.

  • Nasi padang

    A genuinely distinctive way of eating, with a dozen small dishes brought to your table at once and paid for only by what you actually take.

    Where to try it Padang restaurants nationwide, identifiable by their stacked-dish window displays.

  • Indonesian coffee (kopi)

    Genuinely world-renowned coffee country, from Sumatra's earthy Mandailing beans to Bali's plantations and the famously unusual kopi luwak.

    Where to try it Coffee plantations in Bali and Sumatra, and warungs nationwide.

When to go

Best time to visit Indonesia

Indonesia's equatorial climate genuinely means less seasonal temperature variation than many destinations, but the dry-versus-wet season split still shapes a trip significantly.

  • Dry season (April–October)

    Best time

    The genuinely most reliable window for hiking Bromo, diving Raja Ampat and touring Komodo, and also the busiest, most expensive season.

  • Shoulder months (April, October)

    Good

    Genuinely good weather odds with noticeably thinner crowds than peak July–August.

  • Wet season (November–March)

    Good

    Genuinely still workable for Bali and Java, with short, heavy daily downpours rather than constant rain, though rougher seas can disrupt boat trips.

  • Ramadan period

    Good

    Varies each year — some Java restaurants keep shorter daytime hours, though Bali and tourist areas are genuinely largely unaffected.

Worth planning around: Avoid booking boat-heavy Komodo or Raja Ampat trips deep in wet season (December–February), when rough seas most often disrupt island-hopping.

Trip length

How long to spend in Indonesia

Indonesia's scale genuinely makes a single trip covering everything unrealistic — picking two or three regions well beats a rushed island-hopping itinerary.

  • 5–7 days

    Bali alone — Ubud, Seminyak or Kuta, and a day trip or two — the classic short Indonesia trip.

    Genuinely the most popular short itinerary, and complete enough on its own.

  • 8–10 days

    Bali plus Java — add Yogyakarta, Borobudur and Mount Bromo to a Bali base for real cultural and volcanic depth.

    The itinerary on this page, connecting Bali and Java by a short domestic flight.

  • 12–14 days

    Bali, Java and Komodo — add Labuan Bajo and Komodo National Park for dragons and island-hopping by boat.

    A genuinely complete first circuit of Indonesia's most famous sights.

  • 2 weeks or more

    Add Raja Ampat, Lombok's Gili Islands or Lake Toba, both genuinely worth dedicated time rather than a rushed detour.

    Raja Ampat and Lake Toba are where a second Indonesia trip belongs.

Getting around

Transportation in Indonesia

Domestic flights genuinely do most of the heavy lifting in Indonesia — this is an archipelago, and distances between regions are far too large to drive.

  • Domestic flights

    Genuinely essential for connecting Bali, Java, Komodo and Raja Ampat.

    Budget carriers like Lion Air and Citilink connect most major airports affordably.

  • Ride-hailing apps (Gojek, Grab)

    Genuinely the easiest way to get around cities and towns, with fixed upfront pricing and no haggling.

    Widely used in Bali, Java and other major islands; less available on smaller islands.

  • Private driver or car charter

    A genuinely common, affordable way to see Bali or Yogyakarta at your own pace with a local driver.

    Typically booked by the day through a hotel or driver directly.

  • Ferries and liveaboards

    The genuinely standard way to explore Komodo National Park and Raja Ampat's scattered islands.

    Multi-day liveaboard boats are the classic way to see Komodo's islands properly.

  • Trains (Java only)

    A genuinely comfortable, scenic way to move between Java's cities, though the rail network doesn't extend to most other islands.

    Executive-class trains connect Jakarta, Bandung and Yogyakarta reliably.

Culture

Culture and etiquette in Indonesia

Indonesian culture genuinely varies enormously by island and religion, but shares a strong nationwide emphasis on politeness, community and religious respect.

  • Dress modestly at temples and mosques

    A sarong and sash are genuinely required at Balinese temples, usually available to rent or borrow at the entrance; cover shoulders and knees at mosques.

  • Use your right hand

    Genuinely eat, pass items and shake hands with your right hand — the left is traditionally considered unclean.

  • Keep your cool in disagreements

    Public displays of anger or raised voices are genuinely considered a loss of face — calm, indirect communication works far better.

  • Remove shoes before entering homes and some temples

    A genuinely standard courtesy, and often enforced at temple entrances specifically.

  • Respect prayer times and Ramadan

    In Muslim-majority areas, genuinely be mindful of call-to-prayer timings and, during Ramadan, of eating discreetly in public during fasting hours.

  • Tipping is appreciated, not demanded

    Rounding up or leaving a small tip is genuinely a welcome gesture, though never strictly required.

Do

  • Dress modestly and wear a sarong when visiting temples.
  • Bargain politely and with good humour at markets — it's genuinely expected.
  • Carry small rupiah notes for warungs, temples and local transport.
  • Book domestic flights and Komodo or Raja Ampat trips well ahead in peak season.

Don't

  • Don't touch anyone's head, including children's — it's considered genuinely disrespectful.
  • Don't point with your index finger — use your thumb or a whole open hand instead.
  • Don't underestimate travel times between islands — distances here are genuinely much larger than they look on a map.
  • Don't swim at Komodo or Raja Ampat sites without checking currents and tides first.

Travel tips

Indonesia travel tips

The things that most often go wrong on an Indonesia trip, in rough order of how much they cost people.

  • Book domestic flights and any Komodo or Raja Ampat liveaboard well ahead for dry-season travel.
  • Carry your passport or a copy — some island transfers and hotel check-ins genuinely require it.
  • Budget extra time for island transfers — ferries and flights are more weather-dependent than they first appear.
  • Bring reef-safe sunscreen, genuinely required at many marine parks including Raja Ampat.
  • Negotiate driver and tour prices upfront, genuinely before you get in the car or boat.
  • Stay hydrated and pace yourself in the heat, especially on temple visits and volcano treks.
  • Check current volcanic activity advisories before any Bromo or Ijen trek.
  • Keep small rupiah notes on hand — many warungs and local transport don't take cards.

FAQ

Visiting Indonesia: frequently asked questions

How many days do you need in Indonesia?

Ten to fourteen days covers Bali and Java comfortably, which is the itinerary on this page. Five to seven days is enough for Bali alone, and genuinely still worthwhile.

What is the best time of year to visit Indonesia?

April to October, the dry season, for the most reliable weather across Bali, Java, Komodo and beyond; the wet season still works for Bali specifically, with shorter daily downpours.

Do you need to fly between Indonesian islands?

For anywhere beyond a single island, genuinely yes — domestic flights or ferries are the standard way to connect Bali, Java, Komodo and further afield.

Is Indonesia expensive?

No, genuinely one of Southeast Asia's better-value destinations — mid-range travel runs about Rp 800,000–1,600,000 per person per day, with domestic flights and dive trips the main added cost.

Can you see Komodo dragons in the wild?

Yes, genuinely, on Komodo and Rinca islands within Komodo National Park, on a short guided walk with a ranger — no zoo required.

Is Indonesia safe to visit?

Generally yes, genuinely safe for travellers, with petty theft and scams the main everyday risk. Natural hazards — volcanic activity and earthquakes — are a real, ongoing consideration worth checking before remote trips.
