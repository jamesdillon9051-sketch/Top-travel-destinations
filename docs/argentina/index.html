Why go to Argentina

Argentina is the eighth-largest country in the world, stretching about 3,700 km from the subtropical north to the sub-Antarctic south, and from the Andes in the west to the Atlantic in the east. Its people are largely descended from immigrants, particularly Italians and Spaniards, and its capital, Buenos Aires, is one of the most European-feeling cities in South America. It was a Spanish colony until independence in 1816 and became one of the richest countries in the world in the early 20th century, before decades of political and economic turmoil.

For visitors, the range is extraordinary. There is Buenos Aires, with its tango halls, cafés, bookshops and parrillas; Patagonia, with the Perito Moreno Glacier, the granite towers of Fitz Roy and the lakes of Bariloche; the vineyards of Mendoza under the Andes; the coloured canyons and adobe villages of the north-west; the Iguazu Falls; and whales and penguins on the Atlantic coast. It is also a country of food, particularly beef, empanadas and wine, and of a warm, argumentative, passionate culture.

Argentina is not a cheap country in the way it once was, and its economy is volatile, with inflation and shifting exchange rates. Travellers should plan carefully and check current advice on money. But it remains one of the most rewarding countries in South America, and a place where distances are big, landscapes are breathtaking and the welcome is warm.

Experiences

Best things to do in Argentina

The experiences that recur across the country and make an Argentine trip feel like one.

  1. Watch a tango show, or dance one

    Tango was born in the port districts of Buenos Aires and Montevideo, and is danced in milongas, or dance halls, and performed in shows across the city. See Buenos Aires.

    Tip For a local experience, go to a milonga rather than a dinner show.

  2. Walk on or beside a glacier

    Perito Moreno is one of the few advancing glaciers in the world, and its calving ice is a thrilling sight from walkways and boats. See El Calafate.

    Tip Visit early, and add a boat or a guided ice trek.

  3. Hike beneath Fitz Roy

    Some of the best day walks in the world start at El Chaltén, leading to lakes beneath granite spires. See El Chaltén.

    Tip Go early on a clear day, and bring windproof layers.

  4. Taste Malbec in the vineyards

    Argentina's flagship red grape thrives at altitude under the Andes, and many bodegas offer tastings, lunches and stays. See Mendoza.

    Tip Book a driver or a bike tour, and book lunch.

  5. Go whale watching

    Southern right whales gather off the Valdés coast from June to December, with calves close to the boats. See Península Valdés.

    Tip Choose licensed operators, and go in September or October.

  6. Meet a gaucho at an estancia

    A day or night at a working ranch offers horseback riding, an asado barbecue and a glimpse of the gaucho tradition, near Buenos Aires or in Patagonia.

    Tip Book a full-day visit with lunch.

  7. Go to a football match

    Football is a passion, and a match at La Bombonera or the Monumental is an unforgettable experience.

    Tip Book through a reputable tour, since tickets for foreigners are restricted.

Food and drink

Argentina foods to try

Argentine food is beef-centred, with Italian and Spanish influences, and meals are slow, late and social. Dinner rarely starts before 9pm.

  • Asado

    The traditional barbecue: beef ribs, sausages, sweetbreads and other cuts slow-cooked over wood or charcoal, served with chimichurri and salad. It is a weekend ritual and a social occasion.

    Where to try it Parrillas in Buenos Aires and estancias across the country.

  • Empanadas

    Baked or fried pastries filled with beef, chicken, cheese or vegetables, each province with its own style. The Salta and Tucumán versions are famous.

    Where to try it Everywhere, especially in Salta.

  • Provoleta

    A thick slice of provolone cheese, grilled on the parrilla until crisp outside and molten within, and served with oregano and chilli.

    Where to try it Parrillas as a starter.

  • Alfajores

    Two soft biscuits sandwiched with dulce de leche and coated in chocolate or dusted with coconut, the national cookie.

    Where to try it Kiosks and bakeries.

  • Mate

    A bitter herbal infusion of yerba mate, drunk from a gourd through a metal straw and shared among friends. It is more a social ritual than a drink.

    Where to try it In parks, on the road and at home.

  • Locro

    A hearty stew of corn, beans, squash and meat, especially eaten in the north-west and on national holidays.

    Where to try it Restaurants in Salta and Jujuy.

Before you go

Practical travel information for Argentina

The essentials, current for 2026. Argentina is welcoming and varied, but its economy is volatile, so check the latest advice on money, prices and entry.

Capital
Buenos Aires
Language
Spanish, in the Rioplatense dialect, with 'vos' instead of 'tú'. English is spoken in tourist areas, but less than in many countries.
Currency
Argentine peso (ARS). The exchange rate has been volatile, with high inflation, and official and market rates have differed at times. Check the current rate and rules before you go.
Cards and cash
Rules on foreign cards and cash have changed repeatedly. Recently many visitors have paid by card at favourable rates or with cash US dollars, but this can change, so check current advice. Carry some pesos for small purchases, and note that ATMs often limit withdrawals and charge high fees.
Entry and visas
Many nationalities, including most of Europe, the United States, Canada and Australia, can enter without a visa for up to 90 days. Rules change, so check the official government site for your passport.
Power sockets
Types C and I, 220 V, 50 Hz.
Emergency number
911 for police and emergencies, 107 for ambulances and 100 for fire.
Tipping
About 10% in restaurants, and a small tip for guides, drivers and porters. A service charge is not usually included.
Safety
Argentina is generally safe for visitors, but petty theft is common in Buenos Aires, including bag and phone snatching, and pickpocketing on crowded buses and the metro. Keep valuables hidden, use taxi apps, and be alert near stations and tourist areas. Strikes and demonstrations occur, and can disrupt transport. Patagonia and the wine regions are calm.
Internet and SIM
Good in cities and towns, with SIM cards and eSIMs available. Coverage is patchy in Patagonia and the mountains.
Time zone
UTC−3 all year, with no daylight saving time.

When to go

Best time to visit Argentina

The seasons are reversed from the northern hemisphere. Patagonia is best from November to March, Buenos Aires in spring and autumn, Mendoza in autumn and the north-west from April to October.

  • Summer (December–February)

    Best time

    The peak for Patagonia and Ushuaia, with long days and hiking weather. Buenos Aires is hot and humid, and many locals go on holiday.

  • Autumn (March–May)

    Best time

    Warm and settled, with the grape harvest in Mendoza and autumn colour in the Lakes District. Excellent for Buenos Aires.

  • Winter (June–August)

    Good

    Cold in Patagonia and the south, but good for skiing in Bariloche and Mendoza, whales at Valdés and the dry, sunny north-west.

  • Spring (September–November)

    Best time

    Warm, with jacaranda blossoms in Buenos Aires and whales at their best at Valdés. Patagonia warms up from November.

Worth planning around: Patagonia in winter, when many services close, and the peak Christmas–February holiday period if you dislike crowds and high prices.

Trip length

How long to spend in Argentina

Argentina is enormous, and travel involves long flights or bus journeys. Two weeks allows Buenos Aires and Patagonia with one other region, and three weeks adds the north or the wine country.

  • 7–8 days

    Buenos Aires and Iguazu, or Buenos Aires and Patagonia.

    A first taste, with one flight.

  • 12–14 days

    The itinerary on this page: Buenos Aires, Iguazu, El Calafate, El Chaltén and Bariloche.

    A balanced first trip with four flights.

  • 3 weeks

    Adds Mendoza, Salta or Ushuaia.

    A slower, richer trip.

  • 4 weeks or more

    Adds Valdés, the north-west and Iberá.

    For those with time to explore.

Getting around

Transportation in Argentina

Argentina's distances are huge. Flights are the way to cover them, and long-distance buses are comfortable and good for shorter hops.

  • Domestic flights

    Aerolíneas Argentinas, Flybondi and JetSMART fly from Buenos Aires to all the main destinations. Prices vary widely, and foreigners sometimes pay more than locals on some airlines.

    Book early for summer, and consider a domestic air pass.

  • Long-distance buses

    Comfortable coaches with reclining seats and meals, on routes such as Buenos Aires to Mendoza (about 14 hours) and El Calafate to El Chaltén (about 3 hours).

    Choose cama (bed) seats for overnight trips.

  • Hire cars

    Useful for Patagonia, the Lakes District, Mendoza and the north-west. Distances are long and fuel stops are far apart.

    Book insurance carefully, and expect unpaved roads.

  • Taxis and ride-hailing

    Taxis, Cabify and Uber operate in Buenos Aires and other cities.

    Use the app or a radio taxi.

  • Ferries

    Fast ferries link Buenos Aires with Colonia and Montevideo in Uruguay.

    Book in advance in summer.

Culture

Culture and etiquette in Argentina

Argentines are warm, expressive and proud, and social life revolves around family, friends, food and conversation. People kiss on the cheek in greeting, dinner is late, and politics and football are always on the agenda. The country has a strong café and bookshop culture.

  • Greet with a kiss

    A single kiss on the right cheek is normal between women, and between men and women. Men usually shake hands.

  • Eat late

    Restaurants open for dinner at about 8 or 9pm, and people rarely eat before 9. Lunch is around 1 to 2pm.

  • Share mate

    If you are offered mate, accept and return the gourd when you are done. Do not stir the straw.

  • Be patient

    Service can be slow and relaxed. Waiters do not hurry you, and you must ask for the bill.

  • Dress smart-casual

    Argentines dress well, particularly in Buenos Aires. Beachwear is for the beach.

  • Avoid sensitive topics

    The Falkland Islands (Malvinas), politics and the economy are emotive. Listen more than you argue.

Do

  • Learn a few Spanish phrases: hola (hello), gracias (thank you), por favor (please)
  • Arrive late for social occasions, as locals do
  • Try the local wine and beef
  • Carry a photocopy of your passport
  • Book long-distance buses and popular tours in advance
  • Bring layers, especially for Patagonia

Don't

  • Don't leave phones, bags or cameras on tables or seats
  • Don't take unlicensed taxis at night
  • Don't call the Falklands anything other than a sensitive subject
  • Don't rush a meal
  • Don't underestimate distances and weather in Patagonia
  • Don't feed wild animals

Travel tips

Argentina travel tips

The things that most often catch visitors out in Argentina, several of which are specific to how the country works.

  • Check current advice on money and exchange rates before you go. It changes often.
  • Carry some cash, since not every place accepts foreign cards.
  • Book flights, buses and the Perito Moreno and Fitz Roy trips ahead in summer.
  • Keep phones and bags out of sight in Buenos Aires.
  • Take layers and a windproof jacket, even in summer, in Patagonia.
  • Eat dinner late, or you will have restaurants to yourself.
  • Be flexible. Strikes, delays and weather can change plans.
  • Try a parrilla at least once, and share the meat.

FAQ

Visiting Argentina: frequently asked questions

Is Argentina safe?

Generally, yes. Petty theft is common in Buenos Aires, so keep valuables hidden and use taxi apps. Patagonia and the wine regions are very safe.

How many days do you need in Argentina?

Two weeks for a first trip, covering Buenos Aires and Patagonia, and three if you add Mendoza or Salta.

What is the best time to visit Argentina?

It depends where. November to March for Patagonia, spring and autumn for Buenos Aires and Mendoza.

Is Argentina expensive?

It has become more expensive than in the past, though prices are volatile. Meals and wine are still good value, but flights and accommodation can cost a lot.

Do I need a visa for Argentina?

Many nationalities can enter without a visa for up to 90 days. Check the official government site.

Is it easy to travel by bus?

Yes. Long-distance buses are comfortable, and cama-class seats recline almost flat.

Can I do Patagonia and Iguazu in one trip?

Yes, but with flights via Buenos Aires. Allow at least ten days.
