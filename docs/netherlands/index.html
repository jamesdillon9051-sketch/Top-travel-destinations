Why go to Netherlands

The Netherlands is a genuinely small, flat, and famously engineered country — roughly a quarter of it sits below sea level, held back by a centuries-old system of dikes, polders and pumping windmills that the Dutch built out of sheer necessity. That same engineering instinct shaped Amsterdam's concentric canal rings, a 17th-century masterplan now a UNESCO World Heritage Site, and it still shows in a rail network and cycling infrastructure that make getting around the country genuinely effortless.

Beyond Amsterdam, the case for the Netherlands is how much variety fits into such a compact area: Rotterdam's bombed-and-rebuilt skyline of experimental modern architecture, Utrecht's canals sitting a level below street traffic rather than beside it, Delft's blue-and-white pottery tradition and Vermeer's old streets, and a UNESCO windmill landscape at Kinderdijk that looks genuinely unchanged since the 18th century.

Spring adds a layer most visitors plan entire trips around — the Keukenhof gardens and the surrounding bulb fields turn parts of the country into solid blocks of tulip colour for about six weeks each year. A well-developed train network and the world's most bike-friendly infrastructure make combining several of these destinations in one trip genuinely straightforward, even without a car.

Experiences

Best things to do in Netherlands

The experiences that recur across the country and that make a Netherlands trip feel like more than a checklist of canal photos and stroopwafels.

  1. Cycle everywhere

    Genuinely the best way to experience any Dutch city, on a country with more bikes than people and dedicated cycle paths running virtually everywhere.

    Tip Rent from a station-based bike-share or a local shop rather than a tourist-priced kiosk, and always stay out of the red-paved bike lanes if walking.

  2. Ride a canal boat

    Amsterdam's and Utrecht's canals are genuinely best seen from the water, at a completely different perspective from the bridges and towpaths above.

    Tip An early morning or evening cruise avoids the midday crowds and gives noticeably better light for photos.

  3. Visit a working windmill

    Kinderdijk's UNESCO-listed windmills and the historic village at Zaanse Schans both offer a genuinely close look at the pumps and machinery that kept the Netherlands dry for centuries.

    Tip Some windmills at both sites are open to walk inside on set days — check current opening schedules before visiting.

  4. See the tulip fields in bloom

    For roughly six weeks each spring, the bulb fields around Keukenhof turn into genuinely solid blocks of colour, one of the most photographed agricultural sights in Europe.

    Tip Bloom dates shift year to year with the weather — check a current bloom forecast before booking a specific date.

  5. Try Dutch cheese at a historic market

    Alkmaar's cheese market, running since 1593, genuinely re-enacts the traditional trading ritual each week in season, with cheese carriers in period dress and guild colours.

    Tip The market runs Friday mornings in season only — check current dates before planning a special trip.

  6. Go museum-hopping in Amsterdam

    The Rijksmuseum, Van Gogh Museum and Anne Frank House sit within walking distance of each other, a genuinely dense concentration of world-class collections for a city this size.

    Tip Book timed tickets online well ahead for all three — same-day entry is rarely possible in high season.

Food and drink

Netherlands foods to try

Dutch food's reputation abroad rarely does justice to a genuinely serious cheese, herring and street-snack culture found at every price point.

  • Stroopwafel

    Two thin waffles sandwiching a genuinely gooey caramel syrup filling, traditionally warmed by balancing one atop a hot cup of coffee or tea.

    Where to try it Street stalls selling them fresh and warm, especially at markets — genuinely better than the packaged supermarket version.

  • Bitterballen

    Deep-fried, breadcrumbed balls of a genuinely rich beef ragout, a classic Dutch bar snack served with mustard for dipping alongside a beer.

    Where to try it Brown cafés (traditional Dutch pubs) nationwide — a plate of bitterballen with beer is a genuine national ritual.

  • Gouda cheese

    The Netherlands' most famous export, genuinely ranging from mild young wheels to deeply savoury, crystalline aged versions matured for years.

    Where to try it Cheese shops and markets nationwide, with a tasting at the source genuinely worthwhile in Gouda itself.

  • Haring (raw herring)

    Lightly cured raw herring, traditionally eaten holding it by the tail and tipping your head back, chopped onion and pickles on the side — a genuine Dutch institution that surprises most first-time visitors.

    Where to try it Herring stalls (haringkar) found on street corners in every Dutch city, especially around Amsterdam's canals.

  • Patat (Dutch fries)

    Twice-fried potato chips served in a paper cone, genuinely defined by the sauce rather than the fries themselves — mayonnaise is the default, not ketchup.

    Where to try it Independent friteries (frituur) nationwide, genuinely better than any chain.

  • Stamppot

    Mashed potato stamped together with a vegetable — kale is the classic version — and served with rookworst, a genuinely hearty Dutch comfort dish built for winter.

    Where to try it Traditional Dutch restaurants nationwide, especially welcome in the colder months.

Practical travel information for Netherlands

The essentials, current for 2026. The Netherlands is straightforward and exceptionally well organised to travel in, with cycling etiquette and Sunday closures the two practicalities that most often catch visitors out.

When to go

Best time to visit Netherlands

The Netherlands is genuinely workable year-round, but the specific window for bulb season versus warm-weather sightseeing is worth planning around deliberately.

  • Spring (April–May)

    Best time

    The tulip fields and Keukenhof are at their genuine best, with mild temperatures — this is the single most popular window and books up accordingly.

  • Summer (June–August)

    Best time

    Long daylight hours, the warmest weather, and outdoor café and canal-side culture in full swing — also the busiest and most expensive season in Amsterdam.

  • Autumn (September–October)

    Good

    Mild and noticeably quieter than summer, with good light for photography and thinning crowds at the major museums.

  • Winter (November–March)

    Shoulder

    Cold, grey and often windy, though canal-side Christmas markets and, in a genuine cold snap, ice skating on frozen canals offer real winter charm.

Worth planning around: Book Keukenhof and central Amsterdam accommodation many months ahead for the April–May tulip season — this is genuinely the busiest and most tightly booked window of the year.

Trip length

How long to spend in Netherlands

The Netherlands' compact size and excellent train network make an ambitious multi-city itinerary genuinely workable without a car.

  • 3–4 days

    Amsterdam alone fills three or four days comfortably, with an easy day trip to Zaanse Schans or Kinderdijk.

    A short trip is better spent deeply in Amsterdam than spread thin across the country.

  • 7–9 days

    Amsterdam, Utrecht and Delft or The Hague, connected by train — a well-rounded first trip covering the classic Randstad cities.

    Add Kinderdijk or Zaanse Schans as an easy day trip from any of these bases.

  • 10–14 days

    The itinerary on this page: Amsterdam, Zaanse Schans, Utrecht, Delft, The Hague, Rotterdam and Kinderdijk, with a tulip-season detour to Keukenhof.

    Enough time to add Giethoorn or Maastricht for a genuinely different side of the country.

  • 3 weeks or more

    Add Maastricht and Giethoorn in full, both genuinely worth a dedicated day or two rather than a rushed add-on.

    A slower pace through the smaller Randstad towns is where a second Netherlands trip belongs.

Getting around

Transportation in Netherlands

The Netherlands' train network connects every destination in this guide well, and cycling covers the rest — a car is genuinely unnecessary for most itineraries.

  • Intercity trains (NS)

    Fast, frequent and reliable trains connect Amsterdam, Rotterdam, The Hague, Utrecht and Delft in well under an hour each.

    An OV-chipkaart or contactless bank card covers trains, trams, buses and metros nationwide — no separate tickets needed.

  • Cycling

    Genuinely the best way to get around any Dutch city, with dedicated, well-signed cycle paths connecting neighbourhoods and even nearby towns.

    Bike rental is available everywhere; most Dutch cities are flat and compact enough to cross by bike in 20–30 minutes.

  • Trams, metro and buses

    Comprehensive city networks in Amsterdam, Rotterdam and The Hague cover anywhere cycling or walking doesn't.

    Amsterdam's free ferries across the IJ river are a genuinely good way to see the harbour for the price of a regular transit fare.

  • Rental car

    Useful mainly for reaching Giethoorn efficiently or touring the countryside at a flexible pace — not needed for the main cities.

    Driving and parking in central Amsterdam is genuinely more hassle than it's worth; leave the car at the edge of the city if you have one.

  • Regional trains and buses

    Reach Kinderdijk, Zaanse Schans and Keukenhof reliably from Amsterdam or Rotterdam, all easy day trips without a car.

    Dedicated tourist buses to Keukenhof run direct from Amsterdam and Schiphol Airport during bloom season.

Culture

Culture and etiquette in Netherlands

Dutch culture is shaped by a genuine tradition of directness, egalitarianism and practical, unshowy efficiency, with cycling etiquette a real everyday concern for visitors.

  • Dutch directness is genuine, not rudeness

    Blunt, direct communication is a real cultural norm here — visitors used to more indirect cultures sometimes misread it as unfriendliness, when it usually isn't.

  • Never walk in the bike lanes

    The red-paved cycle paths are genuinely serious infrastructure carrying fast-moving commuter traffic — stepping into one without looking is a real safety risk.

  • Splitting the bill is completely normal

    'Going Dutch' genuinely originates here — splitting a restaurant bill evenly among a group is standard practice, not considered awkward or cheap.

  • Punctuality matters

    Being on time for both social plans and reservations is genuinely expected — arriving significantly late without notice is considered impolite.

  • 'Coffeeshop' and 'café' mean different things

    A coffeeshop sells cannabis; a café or 'koffiehuis' serves regular coffee — genuinely worth knowing before you walk in expecting a cappuccino.

  • Many shops close early on Sundays

    Sunday trading hours are genuinely more limited outside central Amsterdam — check opening times before planning a Sunday shopping trip in smaller towns.

Do

  • Learn a few basic Dutch phrases — genuinely appreciated even though almost everyone speaks fluent English.
  • Rent a bike and use it — it's the single best way to experience any Dutch city like a local.
  • Book major museums and Keukenhof online well ahead, particularly in spring and summer.
  • Carry a physical or contactless card — some smaller vendors no longer accept cash at all.

Don't

  • Don't walk or stand in the bike lanes — they carry genuine, fast-moving traffic.
  • Don't assume a 'coffeeshop' sells coffee — it doesn't, in the conventional sense.
  • Don't expect long, lingering restaurant service — Dutch dining tends to be efficient rather than leisurely.
  • Don't rent a car for Amsterdam itself — parking is scarce and expensive, and it's genuinely unnecessary.

Travel tips

Netherlands travel tips

Practical notes on booking, timing and transport that make a real difference to a Netherlands trip.

  • Book Keukenhof, the Anne Frank House and other major sights online well ahead — same-day entry is rarely available in season.
  • Get an OV-chipkaart or use a contactless bank card for all public transport — it covers trains, trams, buses and metros nationwide.
  • Rent a bike for at least one day — it's genuinely the best way to experience any Dutch city.
  • Check tulip bloom forecasts before booking a specific Keukenhof date, since timing shifts with the weather each year.
  • Carry a card rather than relying on cash — many smaller vendors no longer accept it at all.
  • Pack for rain in any season — Dutch weather is genuinely changeable regardless of month.

FAQ

Visiting Netherlands: frequently asked questions

How many days do you need in the Netherlands?

A week covers Amsterdam plus two or three nearby cities comfortably. Ten to fourteen days allows the fuller Randstad route in this guide, plus Kinderdijk and a tulip-season detour to Keukenhof.

Is the Netherlands expensive to visit?

Moderately, by Western European standards — comparable to Germany or France and less expensive than Switzerland or the Nordic countries. Amsterdam itself runs higher than the rest of the country.

Do you need a car in the Netherlands?

No — the train network connects every major destination in this guide efficiently, and cycling or walking covers city-centre distances. A car is mainly useful for reaching Giethoorn or touring the countryside at a flexible pace.

When do the tulips bloom in the Netherlands?

Roughly mid-March to mid-May, peaking in April, though exact dates shift year to year with the weather. Keukenhof publishes a current bloom forecast each season — check it before booking a specific date.

Is Amsterdam safe for visitors?

Yes, genuinely very safe by international standards. The main practical risk is bicycle theft, plus the usual pickpocketing vigilance needed in any crowded tourist area.

What is the best way to get around Dutch cities?

By bicycle, genuinely — the Netherlands has the world's best cycling infrastructure, and renting a bike for at least a day is one of the best ways to experience any Dutch city like a local.
