Why go to Mexico

Mexico is nearly two million square kilometres, and it holds several countries' worth of variety: Aztec Tenochtitlán buried under one of the largest cities on Earth, the pyramids of Teotihuacán and the Maya cities of the Yucatán, silver-boom colonial towns in the central highlands, cloud forest and jungle in the south, and coastlines on the Pacific, the Gulf and the Caribbean. It was the heart of Mesoamerica for three thousand years and the centre of New Spain for three hundred, and both layers are visible everywhere.

It is also a food country to a degree few others are. Mexican cuisine is on UNESCO's list of intangible heritage, and it varies sharply by region: the moles and mezcal of Oaxaca, the cochinita pibil of the Yucatán, the tacos al pastor of Mexico City, the birria of Jalisco and the chiles en nogada of Puebla. Markets, street stalls and family restaurants are as good a reason to travel here as any monument.

Practically, Mexico is easier than its reputation. Long-distance buses are excellent, domestic flights are cheap, and the main tourist routes are well developed. The country is also large and uneven, and safety varies a great deal by state, so read your government's current advice for each region you plan to visit and use the same common sense you would in any big country.

Experiences

Best things to do in Mexico

The experiences that recur wherever you go and that make a Mexican trip feel like one.

  1. Climb a pyramid

    Teotihuacán, Chichén Itzá, Uxmal, Palenque and Monte Albán give you five thousand years of Mesoamerican architecture, and some still let you climb. See Teotihuacán and Chichén Itzá.

    Tip Arrive at opening. The sun is punishing by midday and the tour buses arrive by ten.

  2. Eat at the markets

    Every city has a central market where a plate of tlacoyos, tamales or a bowl of pozole costs a couple of dollars, and where you can see ingredients — dozens of chiles, mole pastes, tropical fruit — that never reach supermarkets.

    Tip Look for stalls with a queue of locals and go before 14:00.

  3. Swim in a cenote

    The Yucatán's limestone is riddled with sinkholes filled with clear, cool groundwater that the Maya considered sacred and that today are the best swimming in the region. See Tulum and Mérida.

    Tip Rinse off before you swim, and use biodegradable sunscreen or none.

  4. Walk a colonial city

    Guanajuato, San Miguel de Allende and Puebla have UNESCO-listed centres of baroque churches, plazas and painted houses built on silver and trade.

    Tip Stay in the centre, so that you can walk the plazas in the evening.

  5. Taste mezcal and tequila

    Mezcal from Oaxaca and tequila from Jalisco are distilled from agave, and visiting the producers shows the craft behind them. See Oaxaca and Tequila.

    Tip Sip it slowly, with orange slices and sal de gusano, and do not shoot it.

  6. Be there for Día de Muertos

    On 1 and 2 November, families build altars, decorate graves with marigolds and spend the night in cemeteries. It is not a Halloween party but a warm, moving celebration of the dead, and Oaxaca and Michoacán are the best places to see it.

    Tip Book accommodation months ahead, and be respectful in cemeteries.

Food and drink

Mexico foods to try

Mexican food is far more regional than the taco-and-burrito version abroad, built on corn, beans, chiles, tomatoes, squash and tropical fruit, with Spanish, African, Middle Eastern and Asian influences. Eat where the locals eat.

  • Tacos al pastor

    Pork marinated in chiles and achiote, stacked on a vertical spit like a shawarma and shaved onto small tortillas with pineapple, onion and coriander. It is the signature taco of Mexico City and a Lebanese immigrant legacy.

    Where to try it Taquerías in Mexico City, open late and everywhere.

  • Mole

    Complex sauces of chiles, spices, seeds, chocolate and sometimes fruit, ground and simmered for hours. Oaxaca claims seven classic moles, and Puebla's mole poblano is the best known.

    Where to try it Family restaurants in Oaxaca and Puebla.

  • Cochinita pibil

    Pork marinated in sour orange and achiote, wrapped in banana leaves and slow-cooked in a pit oven, served in tacos with pickled red onion and habanero salsa.

    Where to try it Markets and taquerías in Mérida.

  • Tlayudas

    Huge crisp tortillas spread with beans and topped with Oaxaca string cheese, meat and salsa, grilled over charcoal. A late-night Oaxacan staple sometimes called a Mexican pizza.

    Where to try it Street stalls and markets in Oaxaca.

  • Birria and pozole

    Birria is spiced goat or beef stew from Jalisco, often served with a broth to dip tacos in; pozole is a hominy and pork soup topped with radish, lettuce and lime.

    Where to try it Restaurants in Guadalajara for birria, and everywhere for pozole.

  • Chiles en nogada

    A seasonal dish, from July to September, of poblano chiles stuffed with meat, fruit and spices, topped with a walnut cream sauce and pomegranate seeds, in the colours of the Mexican flag.

    Where to try it Restaurants in Puebla, where it was invented.

Before you go

Practical travel information for Mexico

The essentials, current for 2026. Mexico is straightforward to travel in, though entry rules and safety advice are worth checking for your nationality and the states you plan to visit.

Capital
Mexico City
Language
Spanish. English is widely spoken in resorts and by younger people in cities, and much less so elsewhere. About 68 indigenous languages are also spoken. A few Spanish phrases go a long way.
Currency
Mexican peso (MXN, written $). Roughly 17–20 pesos to the US dollar in recent years, though it moves.
Cards and cash
Cards are widely accepted in cities and resorts, but carry pesos for markets, taxis, tips and smaller towns. ATMs inside banks are safer than street machines. Always choose to be charged in pesos, not dollars, to avoid poor conversion rates.
Entry and visas
Many nationalities, including the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, the EU and Australia, can visit without a visa for up to 180 days as tourists, and complete a digital immigration form on entry. Rules change, so check the official Mexican government site.
Power sockets
Type A and B, 127 V, 60 Hz, the same as the United States and Canada. Bring an adapter if you come from elsewhere.
Emergency number
911 for all emergencies, nationwide.
Tipping
10–15% in restaurants (check whether service is already added), a few pesos for grocery baggers, gas station attendants and hotel porters, and 10% or so for tour guides.
Safety
Mexico is a large country and safety varies by state. Most tourist areas — Mexico City's main neighbourhoods, Oaxaca, the Yucatán Peninsula, Guanajuato and San Miguel de Allende — are visited by millions each year, and normal precautions apply. Some governments advise against all travel to certain states, particularly along the northern border and in parts of the Pacific coast, and violent crime and cartel activity are serious concerns in those regions. Check your government's advice for every state you plan to visit, use licensed taxis or ride-hailing, avoid night driving on rural roads and follow local guidance.
Internet and SIM
Good 4G and 5G in cities and resorts, patchier in mountains and the jungle. Local SIMs are cheap and eSIMs work. Wi-Fi is common in hotels and cafés.
Time zone
Mexico abolished daylight saving time in 2022. Most of the country is on UTC−6 all year, and the Yucatán Peninsula (Quintana Roo) is on UTC−5. Border cities still follow US daylight-saving.

When to go

Best time to visit Mexico

Mexico's dry season, from November to April, is the best time for most of the country. Summer brings heat on the coasts, humidity and afternoon rain in the highlands, and a hurricane season from June to November on both coasts.

  • Winter and early spring (December–April)

    Best time

    Dry, sunny and comfortable almost everywhere. It is the high season for the beaches and the Maya ruins, with peak prices at Christmas and Easter.

  • Late spring (May–June)

    Shoulder

    Very hot on the coasts and in the lowlands, with the rains starting in June. The central highlands are pleasant, and prices fall.

  • Summer (July–September)

    Quiet

    Rainy and green, with afternoon showers in the highlands and humid heat on the coasts. Hurricanes are most likely in August to October on the Caribbean and Pacific coasts. It is the cheapest time to travel.

  • Autumn (October–November)

    Best time

    The rains end, the countryside is green and the weather turns pleasant. Día de Muertos on 1–2 November is the cultural highlight.

Worth planning around: August to October on the Caribbean and Pacific coasts for hurricane risk, and the days around Easter and Christmas, when domestic travel, prices and crowds peak.

Trip length

How long to spend in Mexico

Mexico is too big to see in a week without an internal flight. Ten days covers two regions well, and two weeks allows three. Distances are long, but flights are cheap and buses are comfortable.

  • 5–7 days

    Mexico City with day trips to Teotihuacán and Puebla, or a week in the Yucatán and Tulum.

    A good short trip focused on one region.

  • 10–12 days

    Mexico City, Oaxaca and either the Yucatán or the colonial highlands, with two internal flights.

    The classic first-time itinerary.

  • 14–16 days

    The itinerary on this page: Mexico City, Puebla, Oaxaca, Mérida and Chichén Itzá, and Tulum.

    Covers the country's main strands: capital, colonial, indigenous and Caribbean.

  • 3 weeks or more

    Adds Guanajuato, San Miguel de Allende, Guadalajara and Chiapas, or the Copper Canyon and the Pacific coast.

    For anyone with time and an appetite for regions off the standard route.

Getting around

Transportation in Mexico

Mexico has an excellent long-distance bus network, cheap domestic flights and, in the south-east, a new tourist railway. Cities have metros, buses and ride-hailing.

  • Domestic flights

    Volaris, Viva Aerobus and Aeroméxico link Mexico City with almost every destination in this guide, often for under US$100. The sensible way to cover long distances.

    Book early, and check baggage rules on the low-cost airlines.

  • First-class buses

    ADO, ETN and other operators run comfortable, air-conditioned coaches with reclining seats and onboard toilets. Mexico City to Puebla is two hours, to Oaxaca about 6–7 hours by day and longer by night.

    Buy tickets online or at the bus terminal, and prefer daytime services.

  • Tren Maya

    A tourist railway connecting Cancún, Tulum, Mérida, Palenque and other Yucatán destinations, opened in stages from 2023.

    Check current routes and timetables, which have changed.

  • Colectivos and local buses

    Shared vans and minibuses link smaller towns and archaeological sites at very low prices, and are the local way of getting around the Yucatán and Oaxaca valleys.

    Ask locals where they leave from, and pay the driver.

  • Taxis and ride-hailing

    Uber and DiDi work in the main cities and are usually safer than street taxis. Airport taxis run on fixed fares.

    Use registered taxis, and agree the fare in advance if there is no meter.

Culture

Culture and etiquette in Mexico

Mexico is warm, formal in its courtesies and proud of its indigenous and mestizo heritage. Family, food and fiestas matter enormously, and politeness — greetings, thanks, a bit of Spanish — goes a long way. Mexico is a devoutly Catholic country with strong indigenous traditions layered on top.

  • Greet people, and say thank you

    Say buenos días, buenas tardes or buenas noches when you enter a shop, a taxi or a restaurant, and por favor and gracias. A handshake is common, and a kiss on the cheek among friends.

  • Tipping is expected

    Service workers earn little, and tips are a large part of their income. Tip in restaurants, taxis (optional), hotels and for guides, and carry small change.

  • Dress modestly in churches

    Cover shoulders and knees in churches and cathedrals, and do not take photographs during services. In villages, dress conservatively.

  • Ask before photographing people

    Many indigenous communities dislike being photographed, and some ban it entirely. Ask first, and respect a refusal.

  • Be careful with water and street food

    Drink bottled or purified water. Street food is usually excellent, particularly at busy stalls where the food is cooked fresh in front of you.

  • Punctuality is flexible

    Social events start later than announced, but tours, buses and flights leave on time.

Do

  • Learn a few Spanish phrases and greet people
  • Tip in restaurants and for services
  • Carry small bills and coins
  • Eat where the locals eat
  • Respect churches, cemeteries and altars
  • Ask permission before photographing people

Don't

  • Don't drink tap water
  • Don't flash valuables or carry more cash than you need
  • Don't take unlicensed taxis at night
  • Don't climb or take anything from archaeological sites
  • Don't buy antiquities or products made from endangered animals
  • Don't drive at night on rural roads

Travel tips

Mexico travel tips

The things that most often catch visitors out in Mexico, several of which are specific to how the country works.

  • Check your government's travel advice state by state. Mexico is a big country, and warnings for some states do not apply to the tourist areas covered in this guide.
  • Fly for long distances and take buses for short ones. Mexico City to Oaxaca or Mérida by flight is an hour or two, and a full day or more by road.
  • Book Chichén Itzá, Teotihuacán and Monte Albán early in the day. They fill with tour buses and get very hot by late morning.
  • Drink bottled or filtered water, but do not worry about ice in restaurants, which is almost always from purified water.
  • Carry pesos. Small vendors, taxis, tips and colectivos need cash, and card payments in dollars carry poor exchange rates.
  • Watch the altitude in Mexico City, which sits at 2,240 metres. Take it easy on the first day, drink water and go light on alcohol.
  • Use Uber or DiDi in cities, and licensed airport taxis. They are safer and avoid haggling.
  • Plan around the holidays. Semana Santa, Christmas and the Día de Muertos weeks book out weeks ahead, and prices rise.

FAQ

Visiting Mexico: frequently asked questions

Is Mexico safe to visit?

Most tourist areas, including Mexico City's main neighbourhoods, Oaxaca, the Yucatán, Guanajuato and San Miguel de Allende, receive millions of visitors each year without problems. Some states, particularly along the northern border and in parts of the Pacific coast, have serious crime and cartel violence, and some governments advise against travel there. Check your government's advice for each state.

How many days do you need in Mexico?

Ten to sixteen for a first trip covering two or three regions. A week covers Mexico City with day trips, or the Yucatán and the Caribbean coast.

What is the best time to visit Mexico?

November to April for dry weather across most of the country. Avoid August to October on the coasts because of hurricanes, though the highlands are green and quiet.

Do I need a visa for Mexico?

Many nationalities can enter without a visa for up to 180 days as tourists. You complete a digital immigration form on entry. Check the official Mexican government site for your passport.

Can I drink the tap water in Mexico?

No. Drink bottled or purified water, and use it to brush your teeth. Restaurants use purified water for ice and cooking.

Is it better to fly or take the bus?

Fly between regions, as the distances are large and flights are cheap. Use first-class buses within regions, which are comfortable and safe by day.

What should I bring to Mexico?

Sun protection, a hat, insect repellent, layers for the highlands, comfortable shoes, cash in pesos and a reusable water bottle. Bring a light rain jacket in summer.
