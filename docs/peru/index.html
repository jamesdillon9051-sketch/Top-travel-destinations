Why go to Peru

Peru is genuinely a country where extraordinary history is never far from the surface — Inca stonework still forms the foundations of colonial buildings in Cusco, and Machu Picchu remains one of the only major ancient wonders the Spanish conquest never found.

That history sits within genuinely dramatic geography, from Andean peaks over 4,000 metres and the world's deepest canyons to the Amazon basin's dense rainforest and a Pacific coastline hiding lines in the desert visible only from the air.

It's also genuinely a country that rewards a slower pace — high altitude in Cusco and Lake Titicaca demands real acclimatisation time, and the distances between the coast, the Andes and the jungle are considerably greater than a map first suggests.

Experiences

Best things to do in Peru

The experiences that recur across the country and that make a Peru trip feel like more than a checklist of ruins.

  1. Watch sunrise over Machu Picchu

    Genuinely the essential Peru experience, watching the first light reveal the Inca citadel's full scale from the classic viewpoint.

    Tip Genuinely book the first entry slot of the day for both the best light and the thinnest crowds.

  2. Ride a reed boat on Lake Titicaca

    Genuinely a living tradition, not a museum piece, visiting the Uros people's floating islands built entirely from woven totora reeds.

    Tip Genuinely combine with a stay on Taquile or Amantaní island for a fuller, less rushed experience.

  3. Hike a stretch of the Inca Trail

    Genuinely one of the world's great treks, following stone paths laid by the Inca themselves toward Machu Picchu.

    Tip Book genuinely months ahead — permits for the classic four-day trail sell out well in advance.

  4. Spot condors over Colca Canyon

    A genuinely thrilling sight, Andean condors riding thermals at eye level from the canyon's rim at Cruz del Cóndor.

    Tip Genuinely arrive by 8am — the condors' morning flights are far more reliable than later in the day.

  5. Sandboard the dunes at Huacachina

    A genuinely thrilling desert detour, racing dune buggies and sandboarding towering dunes around a palm-fringed oasis.

    Tip Genuinely go in the late afternoon for cooler sand and dramatic desert light.

  6. Search for wildlife in the Amazon

    Genuinely one of the most biodiverse places on Earth, with real chances of macaws, monkeys and giant river otters.

    Tip Genuinely a multi-day lodge stay reveals far more wildlife than a rushed day trip.

Food and drink

Peru foods to try

Peruvian food's growing global reputation is genuinely well earned, built on serious seafood, Andean ingredients and centuries of cultural fusion.

  • Ceviche

    Genuinely Peru's national dish, fresh raw fish cured in citrus with onion and chili, best eaten fresh at midday.

    Where to try it Coastal cevicherías nationwide, especially in Lima.

  • Lomo saltado

    A genuinely beloved fusion dish, beef stir-fried with onions, tomatoes and soy sauce, served with fries and rice.

    Where to try it Restaurants nationwide, from street stalls to fine dining.

  • Pisco sour

    Genuinely Peru's national cocktail, a frothy mix of pisco brandy, lime, egg white and bitters.

    Where to try it Bars and restaurants nationwide.

  • Causa limeña

    Genuinely a Lima classic, mashed yellow potato layered with chicken, avocado or seafood, seasoned with lime and chili.

    Where to try it Restaurants throughout Lima and nationwide.

  • Anticuchos

    Genuinely a beloved street food, marinated and grilled beef heart skewers with a real depth of smoky flavour.

    Where to try it Street stalls nationwide, especially in the evening.

  • Coca tea

    Genuinely a common remedy for altitude sickness in the Andes, a mild herbal tea made from coca leaves.

    Where to try it Hotels and cafés throughout Cusco and Puno.

When to go

Best time to visit Peru

Peru's diverse geography genuinely means the best time varies by region, though the dry Andean season suits most first-time itineraries.

  • Dry season (May–September)

    Best time

    Genuinely the best window for Machu Picchu, Cusco and the Andes, with clear skies and reliable trails, also the busiest season.

  • Shoulder months (April, October)

    Good

    Genuinely fewer crowds with still-decent weather odds across the Andes.

  • Wet season (November–March)

    Good

    Genuinely lush and green, though trails can be muddy and the Inca Trail itself closes each February for maintenance.

  • Amazon dry season (June–October)

    Good

    Genuinely the easiest time for jungle lodges and wildlife spotting, with lower water levels concentrating animals near rivers.

Worth planning around: Attempting the classic Inca Trail in February, when it genuinely closes nationwide for annual maintenance.

How long to spend in Peru

Peru's genuinely long distances and high-altitude acclimatisation needs make a rushed itinerary the most common planning mistake.

  • 5–6 days

    Enough for the essential Inca sites, though genuinely rushed for altitude acclimatisation.

  • 7–9 days

  • 10–14 days

Getting around

Transportation in Peru

A mix of domestic flights, trains and buses is genuinely the standard way to see Peru — the country's scale and mountainous terrain make overland travel alone impractical for most itineraries.

  • Domestic flights

    Genuinely essential for connecting Lima to Cusco, Arequipa and Puerto Maldonado.

    Flights are short but genuinely save days compared to overland travel across the Andes.

  • The PeruRail and Inca Rail trains

    Genuinely the only practical way to reach Machu Picchu without hiking.

    Scenic, if pricey, routes run from Cusco and the Sacred Valley to Aguas Calientes.

  • Long-distance buses

    Genuinely comfortable and affordable for routes like Cusco to Puno or Nazca to Ica.

    Overnight buses with reclining seats cover Peru's longer distances well.

  • Private or shared tours

    Genuinely the standard way to reach Rainbow Mountain and Colca Canyon.

    Both involve early starts and high-altitude driving best left to experienced local operators.

  • Taxis and rideshare apps

    Genuinely the easiest way around within Lima and Cusco themselves.

    Widely available and inexpensive in the main cities.

Culture

Culture and etiquette in Peru

Peruvian culture is shaped by a genuine blending of Andean, Spanish colonial and Amazonian traditions, with real, living pride in Inca heritage rather than treating it as distant history.

  • Greetings are genuinely warm

    A handshake is standard, and a light cheek kiss between women or mixed pairs is common in social settings.

  • Altitude is genuinely taken seriously

    Locals expect visitors to rest and hydrate on arrival in Cusco or Puno, not push through symptoms.

  • Bargaining is genuinely expected at markets

    Politely negotiating prices at craft markets is normal, though fixed-price shops don't expect it.

  • Photographing people genuinely requires asking first

    Especially in traditional dress at markets or festivals — a small tip is often appropriate.

  • Tipping is genuinely modest but appreciated

    Around 10% at restaurants, and small tips for guides and porters on tours.

  • Time runs genuinely more relaxed outside Lima

    Tours and buses in rural areas may run behind schedule — build in buffer time.

Do

  • Rest and hydrate on arrival at high altitude before any strenuous activity.
  • Book Machu Picchu tickets and trains well ahead, especially in the June–August dry season.
  • Carry small cash for markets, tips and rural areas with limited card access.
  • Bring layers — Andean days can swing from hot sun to cold within hours.

Don't

  • Don't skip acclimatisation days in Cusco before attempting Rainbow Mountain or the Inca Trail.
  • Don't drink tap water — stick to bottled or purified water nationwide.
  • Don't photograph people without asking first, especially at traditional markets.
  • Don't rush a Peru itinerary — the distances and altitude genuinely demand a realistic pace.

Travel tips

Peru travel tips

The things that most often go wrong on a Peru trip, in rough order of how much they cost people.

  • Book Machu Picchu entry tickets and train tickets well ahead, especially for June–August travel.
  • Spend at least a full day acclimatising in Cusco before any high-altitude activity.
  • Drink coca tea or carry altitude sickness medication if prone to symptoms.
  • Pack layers regardless of season — Andean temperatures swing dramatically between day and night.
  • Book Inca Trail permits months ahead if hiking the classic route — it sells out early.
  • Carry cash for markets and rural areas, even though cities are increasingly card-friendly.
  • Drink only bottled or purified water throughout the trip.
  • Budget realistic travel days between regions — Peru's distances are genuinely larger than they look on a map.

FAQ

Visiting Peru: frequently asked questions

How many days do you need in Peru?

Ten to fourteen days covers Lima, Cusco, the Sacred Valley, Machu Picchu and Lake Titicaca comfortably, which is the itinerary on this page. A week is enough for Lima, Cusco and Machu Picchu alone, and genuinely still worthwhile.

What is the best time of year to visit Peru?

May to September, the dry season, offers the most reliable weather for Machu Picchu and the Andes. The Amazon has its own separate dry season, roughly June to October.

Do I need to worry about altitude sickness?

Genuinely yes in Cusco and Puno, both above 3,300 metres — rest on arrival, stay hydrated, and consider coca tea or medication if you're prone to symptoms.

Is Peru expensive?

Moderate overall, genuinely affordable outside Machu Picchu's own costs — mid-range travel runs about S/300–550 per person per day, with tickets and trains to Machu Picchu the trip's biggest single expense.

Do you need to book Machu Picchu tickets in advance?

Yes, genuinely essential — daily visitor numbers are capped, and tickets, especially for popular time slots, sell out well ahead in peak season.

Is Peru safe to visit?

Generally yes for tourists in the main destinations, with genuinely sensible precautions around petty theft in Lima and Cusco's busier tourist areas.
